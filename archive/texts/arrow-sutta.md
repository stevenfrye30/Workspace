# The Shorter Discourse With Māluṅkya (MN 63)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Middle Discourses* (Majjhima Nikāya) 63, *Cūḷamālukya Sutta*, "The Shorter Discourse With Māluṅkya" — the simile of the arrow. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The full discourse is held in the Archive library under `middle-discourses-sujato`.

Suppose someone were to say this: ‘I will not lead the spiritual life under the Buddha until the Buddha declares to me that the cosmos is eternal, or that the cosmos is not eternal … or that after death a realized one neither still exists nor no longer exists.’ That would still remain undeclared by the Realized One, and meanwhile that individual would die.

Suppose a man was struck by an arrow thickly smeared with poison. His friends and colleagues, relatives and kin would get a surgeon to treat him. But the man would say: ‘I won’t extract this arrow as long as I don’t know whether the man who wounded me was an aristocrat, a brahmin, a peasant, or a menial.’ He’d say: ‘I won’t extract this arrow as long as I don’t know the following things about the man who wounded me: his name and clan; whether he’s tall, short, or medium; whether his skin is black, brown, or dingy; and what village, town, or city he comes from. I won’t extract this arrow as long as I don’t know whether the bow that wounded me was straight or recurved; whether the bow-string is made of swallow-wort fibre, sunn hemp fibre, sinew, sanseveria fibre, or spurge fibre; whether the shaft is made from a bush or a plantation tree; whether the shaft was fitted with feathers from a vulture, a heron, a hawk, a peacock, or a stork; whether the shaft was bound with sinews of a cow, a buffalo, a black lion, or an ape; and whether the arrowhead was spiked, razor-tipped, barbed, made of iron or a calf’s tooth, or lancet-shaped.’ That man would still not have learned these things, and meanwhile they’d die.

In the same way, suppose someone was to say: ‘I will not lead the spiritual life under the Buddha until the Buddha declares to me that the cosmos is eternal, or that the cosmos is not eternal … or that after death a realized one neither still exists nor no longer exists.’ That would still remain undeclared by the Realized One, and meanwhile that individual would die.

## Atlas notes

The monk Māluṅkyaputta grows frustrated that the Buddha will not pronounce on a fixed set of speculative questions — whether the cosmos is eternal or not, finite or infinite, whether the soul and body are the same, whether an awakened one exists after death — and resolves to leave the order unless they are answered. The Buddha replies with the most famous parable of pragmatism in the canon: a man shot with a poisoned arrow who insists on knowing the caste, name, height, complexion, and home town of his attacker, and the exact make of the bow, string, shaft, feathers, and arrowhead, before he will let the surgeon pull it out — and dies with the questions still open. The spiritual life, the Buddha says, does not depend on settling these matters; whatever view one holds, "there is rebirth, there is old age, there is death, and there is sorrow," and it is the ending of *those* that he teaches. The arrow is suffering; the surgery is the path; the questions are a fatal distraction.

## Context

The *Cūḷamālukya Sutta* belongs to the Majjhima Nikāya and is the locus classicus for the "undeclared points" (*avyākata*) — the questions the Buddha consistently set aside with what the tradition calls his "noble silence." It is the natural companion to the *Sīsapāvana Sutta* (SN 56.31, the handful of leaves), which makes the same case by a gentler image. Together they define a stance often described as the Buddha's therapeutic or pragmatic method: the teaching is calibrated to a single end, the cessation of suffering, and questions that do not serve that end are simply not the work.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the full discourse is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read it as triage, not anti-philosophy. The Buddha is not saying the questions are meaningless or that thinking is bad; he is saying that a person in mortal danger should attend first to the danger. The list of arrow-details is deliberately absurd and exhaustive — it mirrors how endlessly the speculative questions can be pursued — and the punchline is always the same: "meanwhile that individual would die." The discourse asks where one's urgency truly lies.
