# The Vinaya Mahāvagga I: After the Awakening

## Primary Text

**Selected passages** — from the First Khandhaka of the Mahāvagga, the opening of the Vinaya's narrative (chapters and sections are the source's, cited as Mahāvagga I.〈chapter〉.〈section〉; gaps in the section numbers mark omitted sections). T. W. Rhys Davids and Hermann Oldenberg, trans., *Vinaya Texts*, Part I, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XIII (Oxford, the Clarendon Press, 1881); public domain; sourced from the clean Internet Sacred Text Archive HTML (via the Internet Archive Wayback Machine). The English is reproduced verbatim; the translators' footnotes and bracketed page-markers are omitted, and an ellipsis (…) marks an omitted passage or an internal cut. Their italic-letter transliteration is given in standard form — long vowels with macrons, and ñ, ṇ — so their "Nerañgarâ," "Brâhmana," and "samana" appear here as Nerañjarā, Brāhmaṇa, and samaṇa.

**Mahāvagga I, 1.**

**1.** At that time the blessed Buddha dwelt at Uruvelā, on the bank of the river Nerañjarā, at the foot of the Bodhi tree (tree of wisdom), just after he had become Sambuddha. And the blessed Buddha sat cross-legged at the foot of the Bodhi tree uninterruptedly during seven days, enjoying the bliss of emancipation.

**3.** Knowing this the Blessed One then on that occasion pronounced this solemn utterance: 'When the real nature of things becomes clear to the ardent, meditating Brāhmaṇa, then all his doubts fade away, since he realises what is that nature and what its cause.'

**Mahāvagga I, 5.**

**2.** Then in the mind of the Blessed One, who was alone, and had retired into solitude, the following thought arose: 'I have penetrated this doctrine which is profound, difficult to perceive and to understand, which brings quietude of heart, which is exalted, which is unattainable by reasoning, abstruse, intelligible (only) to the wise. This people, on the other hand, is given to desire, intent upon desire, delighting in desire. …'

**6.** And Brahmā Sahampati adjusted his upper robe so as to cover one shoulder, and putting his right knee on the ground, raised his joined hands towards the Blessed One, and said to the Blessed One: 'Lord, may the Blessed One preach the doctrine! may the perfect One preach the doctrine! there are beings whose mental eyes are darkened by scarcely any dust; but if they do not hear the doctrine, they cannot attain salvation. These will understand the doctrine.'

**11.** As, in a pond of blue lotuses, or water-roses, or white lotuses, some … do not emerge over the water, but thrive hidden under the water; and other … reach to the surface of the water; and other … stand emerging out of the water, and the water does not touch them,—

**12.** Thus the Blessed One, looking over the world with his eye of a Buddha, saw beings whose mental eyes were darkened … and when he had thus seen them, he addressed Brahmā Sahampati in the following stanza: 'Wide opened is the door of the Immortal to all who have ears to hear; let them send forth faith to meet it. The Dhamma sweet and good I spake not, Brahmā, despairing of the weary task, to men.'

**Mahāvagga I, 6.**

**6.** Now the Blessed One thought: 'Where do the five Bhikkhus dwell now?' And the Blessed One saw by the power of his divine, clear vision, surpassing that of men, that the five Bhikkhus were living at Benares, in the deer park Isipatana. And the Blessed One, after having remained at Uruvelā as long as he thought fit, went forth to Benares.

**30.** And as the Blessed One had founded the Kingdom of Truth (by propounding the four Noble Truths), the earth-inhabiting devas shouted: 'Truly the Blessed One has founded at Benares, in the deer park Isipatana, the highest kingdom of Truth, which may be opposed neither by a Samaṇa nor by a Brāhmaṇa, neither by a deva, nor by Māra, nor by Brahma, nor by any being in the world.'

**Mahāvagga I, 11.**

**1.** And the Blessed One said to the Bhikkhus: 'I am delivered, O Bhikkhus, from all fetters, human and divine. You, O Bhikkhus, are also delivered from all fetters, human and divine. Go ye now, O Bhikkhus, and wander, for the gain of the many, for the welfare of the many, out of compassion for the world, for the good, for the gain, and for the welfare of gods and men, Let not two of you go the same way, Preach, O Bhikkhus, the doctrine which is glorious in the beginning, glorious in the middle, glorious at the end, in the spirit and in the letter; proclaim a consummate, perfect, and pure life of holiness. … And I will go also, O Bhikkhus, to Uruvelā, to Senāninigama, in order to preach the doctrine.'

## Atlas notes

This is where the story the *Buddha-karita* tells comes to rest and the religion begins. The poem ends with the awakening under the tree; the Vinaya — the "discipline," the code of the monastic order — opens its great narrative (the Mahāvagga, "Greater Division") at the very same spot, then carries the new Buddha forward into the world. Four moments give the passage its shape. First, the **stillness**: seven days seated at the foot of the Bodhi tree by the river Nerañjarā, "enjoying the bliss of emancipation." Second, the **hesitation** — the most humanly surprising episode in the Buddha's life. Surveying what he has understood, he is inclined *not* to teach: the truth is "profound… intelligible only to the wise," and the world is "given to desire"; to preach it might be only "weariness." It takes the god **Brahmā Sahampati**, kneeling, to beg him to "open the door of the Immortal," urging that "there are beings whose mental eyes are darkened by scarcely any dust" who will understand if only they hear. Third, the **decision**: the Buddha looks over the world "with his eye of a Buddha" and sees humanity as a lotus-pond — some flowers still sunk in the water, some rising to the surface, some standing clear of it — and assents, *"Wide opened is the door of the Immortal."* Fourth, the **mission**: having taught the five ascetics at Benares and gathered the first community, he sends them out with the charter of all Buddhist teaching — *"wander, for the gain of the many, for the welfare of the many… Let not two of you go the same way."* (In the source the first disciples are also identified by the closing of each episode; here only the founding moments are quoted.)

## Context

The *Mahāvagga* is the first book of the Khandhaka, the second great division of the Vinaya Piṭaka — the Pali Canon's collection of monastic discipline. Unlike the rule-lists that fill most of the Vinaya, its First Khandhaka frames the rules of ordination with a continuous biography: the awakening, the first teaching, the first conversions, and the formation of the Saṅgha. This same post-awakening sequence is told, with variations, across the canon (in the Ariyapariyesanā Sutta, MN 26) and in later poetic lives such as Aśvaghoṣa's; the Vinaya's version is the oldest connected narrative of the founding of the order. The translation reproduced here is the landmark first English version by T. W. Rhys Davids and Hermann Oldenberg, made from the Pali for the *Sacred Books of the East* (1881); their chapter-and-section numbering (Mahāvagga I.1, I.5, and so on) has been standard ever since.

## Reading note

The episode worth sitting with is the hesitation. A tradition could easily have shown its founder springing up to teach the moment he awakened; instead the oldest sources preserve a Buddha who nearly *keeps the truth to himself*, judging it too deep for a desire-bound world — and who is moved to teach not by triumph but by compassion, once he sees that a few "with little dust in their eyes" can be reached. The lotus-pond is the hinge: it lets him see the world not as uniformly hopeless but as graded, some nearer the surface than others, and so makes teaching worthwhile. Read the sending-forth in that light. "Wander for the welfare of the many" is not a conqueror's command but the practical form of the same compassion that overcame the silence — and "let not two of you go the same way" is its first logistics: cover more ground, reach more of the dust-free eyes, before they are lost.
