# The Middle Between Existence and Non-Existence (SN 12.15)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Linked Discourses* (Saṃyutta Nikāya) 12.15, *Kaccānagotta Sutta*. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `linked-discourses-sujato`.

Then Venerable Kaccānagotta … said to him: “Sir, they speak of this thing called ‘right view’. How is right view defined?”

“Kaccāna, this world mostly relies on the duality of existence and non-existence. But for one who truly sees the origin of the world with right understanding, the concept of non-existence regarding the world does not occur. And for one who truly sees the cessation of the world with right understanding, the concept of existence regarding the world does not occur.

This world is for the most part shackled by getting involved, grasping, and insisting. But if … they do not get attracted, grasp, and fixate on the thought, ‘my self’, they’ll have no doubt or uncertainty that what arises is just suffering arising, and what ceases is just suffering ceasing. Their knowledge of this is independent of others. This is how right view is defined.

‘All exists’: this is one extreme. ‘All does not exist’: this is the second extreme. Avoiding these two extremes, the Realized One teaches by the middle way: ‘Ignorance is a requirement for choices. Choices are a requirement for consciousness. … That is how this entire mass of suffering originates. When ignorance fades away and ceases with no residue left behind, choices cease. … That is how this entire mass of suffering ceases.’”

## Atlas notes

This is one of the shortest discourses in the canon and one of the most philosophically far-reaching — the very text the Mahāyāna philosopher Nāgārjuna singled out, centuries later, as the Buddha's own authority for the doctrine of emptiness. The monk Kaccāna asks plainly: what is "right view"? The Buddha answers by naming the trap the world falls into — the "duality of existence and non-existence," the deep assumption that anything must either solidly *be* or flatly *not be*. But one who really sees how things arise will not say "it does not exist" — for here it is, coming to be; and one who really sees how things cease will not say "it exists" — for there it goes, passing away. So "all exists" is one extreme and "all does not exist" is the other, and the Buddha "teaches by the middle": not being, not non-being, but dependent arising and ceasing. The personal key is not-self — let go of fixating on "my self," and what remains is simply *suffering arising* and *suffering ceasing*, seen directly and "independent of others." It is the canon's most explicit statement that the middle way is not only a path of conduct, between indulgence and self-torment, but a way of seeing, between the reifying of things and their denial.

## Context

The discourse belongs to the Nidānasaṃyutta, the connected discourses on causation, and is the seed-text of the Madhyamaka, the "Middle Way" school. Nāgārjuna quotes it directly in his *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* as proof that the Buddha rejected both "it exists" and "it does not exist," and built upon it his reading of dependent origination as emptiness (*śūnyatā*). It is therefore one of the clearest bridges in the whole tradition between the early Pali teaching and the Perfection of Wisdom — the line that runs from this short exchange to the Heart and Diamond Sūtras.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Hold the two senses of "middle way" together. The first sermon's middle way is practical — between sensual indulgence and self-mortification. This one is conceptual — between "things truly exist, as fixed essences" and "things do not exist at all." Both are the same gesture of non-grasping: do not cling to pleasure or to pain, and do not cling to being or to non-being. Read it as the hinge between the early teaching and the Mahāyāna — dependent origination, followed all the way through, is what the Perfection of Wisdom will call emptiness.
