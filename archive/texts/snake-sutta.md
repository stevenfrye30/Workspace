# The Snake (Sutta Nipāta 1.1)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Anthology of Discourses* (Sutta Nipāta) 1.1, *Uraga Sutta*, "The Serpent." SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `sutta-nipata-sujato`.

When anger surges, they drive it out,
as with medicine a snake's spreading venom.
Such a mendicant sheds the near shore and the far,
as a serpent its old worn-out skin.

They've cut off greed entirely,
like a lotus plucked flower and stalk.
Such a mendicant sheds the near shore and the far,
as a serpent its old worn-out skin.

They've cut off craving entirely,
drying up that swift-flowing stream.
Such a mendicant sheds the near shore and the far,
as a serpent its old worn-out skin.

They've swept away conceit entirely,
as a fragile bridge of reeds by a great flood.
Such a mendicant sheds the near shore and the far,
as a serpent its old worn-out skin.

In future lives they find no worth,
as an inspector of fig trees finds no flower.
Such a mendicant sheds the near shore and the far,
as a serpent its old worn-out skin.

They have no underlying tendencies at all,
and are rid of unskillful roots.
Such a mendicant sheds the near shore and the far,
as a serpent its old worn-out skin.

They've given up the five hindrances,
untroubled, rid of indecision, free of thorns.
Such a mendicant sheds the near shore and the far,
as a serpent its old worn-out skin.

## Atlas notes

The *Uraga Sutta* opens the Sutta Nipāta, and it works by a single, patient image. A snake, when its skin grows old and tight, sloughs it off and slips away, leaving the husk behind. So too the practitioner who has uprooted a defilement — anger, greed, craving, conceit, the deep "underlying tendencies," the five hindrances — "sheds the near shore and the far." The phrase *the near shore and the far* is the canon's shorthand for the whole field of grasping: this world and the next, self and other, the pairs of opposites the mind clings to and is pulled between. To shed both is to stand beyond "here" and "there" altogether. The poem simply runs its refrain through verse after verse, each naming one more fetter cut, so the cumulative effect is of a being molting skin after skin until nothing is held and there is nothing left to leave.

## Context

The discourse is the first text of the Sutta Nipāta, in the *Uragavagga* ("Snake Chapter") of the Khuddaka Nikāya — among the oldest and most spare poetry in the Pali canon. Its central image of crossing beyond "near and far shore" is kin to the Sūtra Nipāta's wider concern with the sage who has gone beyond, and to the famous raft simile elsewhere in the canon. It sits in the same early anthology as the beloved Mettā, Maṅgala, and Ratana suttas.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Let the snake-image stay exact. The skin is not torn off in a struggle; it is outgrown and shed, and then simply left — not carried along out of habit or regret. Read each stanza as one molt: liberation here is not a single dramatic event but the serial, almost natural letting-go of everything that once fit the self and no longer does. The refrain's steadiness is the teaching — the same release, again and again, until both shores are behind.

## Related

- **leaving what carried you** → The Simile of the Raft (MN 22) — having crossed, the raft is left at the shore, as the snake leaves its skin.
- **what is shed** → Dependent Origination (SN 12.1–2) — craving, grasping, and the underlying tendencies named here are the very links by which suffering is built.
