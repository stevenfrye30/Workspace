# Mindfulness of Breathing (MN 118)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Middle Discourses* (Majjhima Nikāya) 118, *Ānāpānasati Sutta*, "Mindfulness of Breathing" — the sixteen steps. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The full discourse, which shows how these steps fulfill the four foundations of mindfulness and the seven awakening factors, is held in the Archive library under `middle-discourses-sujato`.

And how is mindfulness of breathing developed and cultivated to be very fruitful and beneficial? It’s when a mendicant—gone to a wilderness, or to the root of a tree, or to an empty hut—sits down cross-legged, sets their body straight, and brings mindfulness to the present. Just mindful, they breathe in. Mindful, they breathe out.

Breathing in heavily they know: ‘I’m breathing in heavily.’ Breathing out heavily they know: ‘I’m breathing out heavily.’ When breathing in lightly they know: ‘I’m breathing in lightly.’ Breathing out lightly they know: ‘I’m breathing out lightly.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in experiencing the whole body.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out experiencing the whole body.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in stilling the physical process.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out stilling the physical process.’

They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in experiencing rapture.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out experiencing rapture.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in experiencing bliss.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out experiencing bliss.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in experiencing the mental process.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out experiencing the mental process.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in stilling the mental process.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out stilling the mental process.’

They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in experiencing the mind.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out experiencing the mind.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in gladdening the mind.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out gladdening the mind.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in immersing the mind in samādhi.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out immersing the mind in samādhi.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in freeing the mind.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out freeing the mind.’

They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in observing impermanence.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out observing impermanence.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in observing fading away.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out observing fading away.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in observing cessation.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out observing cessation.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe in observing letting go.’ They practice like this: ‘I’ll breathe out observing letting go.’

## Atlas notes

The *Ānāpānasati Sutta* is the canon's complete manual for meditation on the breath — the practice the texts say the Buddha himself used on the night of his awakening, and the one he kept up through his life. Its core is sixteen steps, arranged in four tetrads, reproduced above. The first tetrad settles the **body**: knowing the breath long or short, sensitive to the whole body, calming the bodily process. The second works with **feeling**: breathing with rapture, with bliss, sensitive to and then calming the mental process. The third attends to the **mind**: experiencing it, gladdening it, steadying it in *samādhi*, freeing it. The fourth turns to **insight**: breathing while watching impermanence, fading away, cessation, and letting go. The genius of the scheme is its continuity — a single object, the breath, carries the meditator all the way from first sitting down to the contemplations that issue in liberation. The full discourse then shows that these four tetrads exactly fulfill the four foundations of mindfulness, and that mindfulness in turn fulfills the seven factors of awakening and finally knowledge and freedom.

## Context

The discourse has a memorable frame: the Buddha, pleased with his community after a long rains retreat near Sāvatthī, extends the retreat and gives this teaching by the light of the full moon to an assembly of senior monks. It belongs to the Majjhima Nikāya and stands as the companion to the *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (MN 10): where that text lays out the four foundations as fields of observation, this one shows how all four can be developed through the breath alone. Across the Buddhist meditative traditions — Theravada *vipassanā* and *samatha*, and the breath practices of later schools — these sixteen steps remain the classical template.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the full discourse is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the sixteen steps as a progression you grow into, not a checklist to rush. Early on, the instructions are simply to *know* the breath as it is — heavy or light, long or short — without managing it; calm and sensitivity come on their own as attention steadies. Only in the later tetrads does the practice turn deliberately toward gladdening and freeing the mind, and finally toward the insight contemplations (impermanence, fading, cessation, letting go) that the whole sequence has been preparing. The breath is never left behind: it remains the thread through all sixteen.
