# The Real Miracle, and Where the World Ends (DN 11)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Long Discourses* (Dīgha Nikāya) 11, *Kevaṭṭa (Kevaddha) Sutta*. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `long-discourses-sujato`.

*The three "demonstrations," when the Buddha is pressed to win converts by miracles:*

The demonstration of psychic power, the demonstration of revealing, and the demonstration of instruction.

“Seeing this drawback in psychic power, I'm horrified, repelled, and disgusted by demonstrations of psychic power. … Seeing this drawback in revealing, I'm horrified, repelled, and disgusted by demonstrations of revealing.”

And what is the demonstration of instruction? It's when a mendicant instructs others …

*A monk travels the heavens asking god after god, "where do the four principal states cease without remainder — earth, water, fire, and air?"; none can answer, and he is sent back to the Buddha, who reframes the question:*

“This is how the question should be asked: ‘Where do water and earth, fire and air have no footing; … cease no residue left behind?’ And the answer to that is:

‘Consciousness where no form appears,
infinite, luminous all-round.’

Regarding this, water and earth, fire and air have no footing; … Regarding this, name and form ceases with no residue left behind— with the cessation of consciousness, they cease in reference to this.”

## Atlas notes

Two famous teachings share this discourse, joined by one question: where does real power lie? In the first, a householder named Kevaḍḍha keeps urging the Buddha to have a monk perform public miracles, to impress the town into faith. The Buddha refuses, and explains. There are three kinds of "marvel": psychic power — flying, walking on water, multiplying the body; revealing — reading others' minds; and instruction — teaching. Of the first two he says he is "horrified, repelled, and disgusted" — not because they are impossible, but because to an onlooker they are indistinguishable from mere magic charms and convince no one of anything that matters; a skeptic simply says, "he used a spell." Only the third is a true marvel: the teaching that actually changes a person from the inside. The Buddha's one miracle is transformation, not spectacle.

The second teaching is among the strangest and loveliest passages in the canon. A monk, gripped by the question *where do the four great elements cease without remainder?*, uses psychic power to climb through the heavens, putting it to god after god. None can answer; each sends him higher; at last he reaches Mahā Brahmā, who thunders that he is the Almighty, the Maker — and then, drawn quietly aside, confesses he has no idea, but dares not admit it before his retinue, and packs the monk off back to the Buddha. The Buddha observes, mildly, that the question was wrongly put, and reframes it — and his answer points not outward to the rim of the cosmos but inward, to the mind: it is "where consciousness is signless, infinite, luminous all round" that earth, water, fire, and air, and name-and-form itself, find no footing and cease. The end of the world is not a place one can fly to; it is found where the conditioned mind comes to rest.

## Context

DN 11 is famous on two counts: for the Buddhist attitude to the miraculous — *instruction over spectacle*, which set the tradition's lasting suspicion of powers shown off for effect — and for the scene in which the highest god of the cosmos cannot answer a monk's question and must hide his ignorance, one of the canon's pointed dismissals of a creator deity's authority. Its closing verse on "luminous consciousness" (*viññāṇaṃ anidassanaṃ*) is one of the most discussed and contested lines in the entire Pali canon.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the two halves as one lesson: stop looking for power or truth in the wrong direction — outward, upward, in spectacle or in gods. The marvel worth having is the one that changes you; the "end of the world" worth finding is the cessation reached within. Even the supreme god turns out, here, to be just another being who does not know — and the real authority is not a throne in the highest heaven but the teaching that works. As for the luminous-consciousness verse, the tradition has never fully settled it; read it less as a doctrine to pin down than as a finger pointing past the elements and past name-and-form, to where they no longer find a foothold.
