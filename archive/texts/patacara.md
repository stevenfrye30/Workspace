# Paṭācārā: The Lamp Goes Out (Therīgāthā 5.10)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Verses of the Senior Nuns* (Therīgāthā) 5.10, the verses of Paṭācārā. SuttaCentral, 2019. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `therigatha-sujato`.

Plowing the fields,
sowing seeds in the ground,
providing for partners and children,
young men acquire wealth.

I am accomplished in ethics,
and I do the Teacher's bidding,
being neither lazy nor restless—
why then do I not achieve quenching?

Having washed my feet,
I took note of the water,
seeing the foot-washing water
flowing from high ground to low.

My mind became serene,
like a fine thoroughbred steed.
Then, taking a lamp,
I entered my dwelling,
inspected the bed,
and sat on my cot.

Then, grabbing the pin,
I drew out the wick.
The liberation of my heart
was like the quenching of the lamp.

## Atlas notes

Paṭācārā is remembered as one of the foremost of the Buddha's women disciples, and her story is the tradition's most harrowing account of grief turned to freedom. The commentaries tell the disaster that brought her to the Buddha: in a single stretch of days she lost everyone — her husband to a snakebite, one newborn son swept away by a flooded river and the other carried off by a hawk, and her parents and brother crushed when their house collapsed. Mad with grief, she wandered until her clothes fell from her, and so came at last into the Buddha's presence; he steadied her — "recover your presence of mind, sister" — and her sanity returned, and then her practice. Her own verses, preserved here, do not retell that catastrophe; they tell its quiet ending. After long, faithful effort that somehow would not ripen — *"I am accomplished in ethics… why then do I not achieve quenching?"* — the breakthrough comes from the smallest of things. Washing her feet, she watches the water run downhill and soak away, and her mind grows still. That night, taking a lamp into her room and drawing out the wick to put it out, she is freed: *"the liberation of my heart was like the quenching of the lamp."* The woman undone by deaths finds, in a flame going gently out, the very image of the deathless — for *nibbāna* means, literally, "extinguishment," a lamp quietly gone out.

## Context

Paṭācārā's verses are Therīgāthā 5.10; the Buddha declared her foremost among the nuns in mastery of the monastic discipline (*Vinaya*). Her grief-narrative, preserved in the commentaries, is among the most famous in all of Buddhist literature, and is often read beside the story of Kisā Gotamī and the mustard seed as the tradition's great teachings on bereavement. The water-and-lamp awakening is one of the loveliest moments of insight in the whole Thera- and Therī-gāthā.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Notice what finally frees her: not a grand insight wrenched out by effort, but attention to the most ordinary things — water running downhill, a wick drawn from a flame. The verses quietly correct the striver's complaint of their second stanza ("I do everything right, so why no result?"): liberation is not forced into being but *noticed*, once the mind is still enough to see. And the closing image is exact. A woman wrecked by the deaths of everyone she loved discovers freedom in the gentlest of extinguishings — a small lamp she herself puts out — finding the deathless, at last, in a little darkness she no longer fears.
