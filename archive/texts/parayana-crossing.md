# The Pārāyana: Crossing Over (Sutta Nipāta, Book 5)

## Primary Text

**Selected passages** — three of the sixteen question-and-answer sections of the Pārāyana (Book 5 of the Sutta Nipāta). Ajita's are abridged (verses 1–6 of 8); Upasīva's are complete; Mogharāja's begin at his third question (verses 3–4). Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Anthology of Discourses* (Sutta Nipāta) 5.2 (The Questions of Ajita), 5.7 (The Questions of Upasīva), and 5.16 (The Questions of Mogharāja). SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). Verse numbers are Sujato's; gaps in the numbering and a leading ellipsis mark omitted verses, and his secondary line-break markers (`<j>`) are rendered as spaces. The Pali root text is held alongside in the Archive library under `sutta-nipata-sujato`.

**Sutta Nipāta 5.2 — The Questions of Ajita.**

**1.** "By what is the world shrouded?"
said Venerable Ajita.
"Why does it not shine?
Tell me, what is its sullying?
What is its greatest fear?"

**2.** "The world is shrouded in ignorance,"
replied the Buddha.
"Avarice and negligence make it not shine.
Muttered prayer is its sullying, I say.
Suffering is its greatest fear."

**3.** "The streams flow everywhere,"
said Venerable Ajita.
"What is there to block them?
And tell me the restraint of streams—
by what are they locked out?"

**4.** "The streams in the world,"
replied the Buddha,
"are blocked by mindfulness.
I tell you the restraint of streams—
they are locked out by wisdom."

**5.** "That wisdom and mindfulness,"
said Venerable Ajita,
"and that which is name and form, good fellow;
when questioned, please tell me of this:
where does this all cease?"

**6.** "This question which you have asked,
I shall answer you, Ajita.
Where name and form
cease with no residue left behind—
with the cessation of consciousness,
here they cease."

…

**Sutta Nipāta 5.7 — The Questions of Upasīva.**

**1.** "Alone and independent, O Sakyan,"
said Venerable Upasīva,
"I am not able to cross the great flood.
Tell me a support, All-seer,
depending on which I may cross this flood."

**2.** "Mindfully contemplating nothingness,"
replied the Buddha,
"depending on the perception 'there is nothing', cross the flood.
Giving up sensual pleasures, refraining from chatter,
watch day and night for the ending of craving."

**3.** "One who is free of desire for sensual pleasures,"
said Venerable Upasīva,
"depending on nothingness, all else left behind,
freed in the ultimate liberation of perception:
might they remain there without travelling on?"

**4.** "One free of desire for sensual pleasures,"
replied the Buddha,
"depending on nothingness, all else left behind,
freed in the ultimate liberation of perception:
they might remain there without travelling on."

**5.** "If they were to remain there without travelling on,
even for myriad years, All-seer,
and, being freed, were to grow cool right there,
would the consciousness of that sort pass away?"

**6.** "As a flame tossed by a gust of wind,"
replied the Buddha,
"comes to an end and cannot be reckoned;
so too, a sage freed from the set of mental phenomena
comes to an end and cannot be reckoned."

**7.** "One who has disappeared—do they not exist?
Or are they free from disease for eternity?
Please, sage, answer me clearly,
for truly you understand this matter."

**8.** "One who has disappeared cannot be defined,"
replied the Buddha.
"They have nothing by which others might describe them.
When all things have been eradicated,
eradicated, too, are all ways of speech."

**Sutta Nipāta 5.16 — The Questions of Mogharāja.**

**3.** "So I have come seeking with a question
for the one of excellent vision.
How to look upon the world
so the King of Death won't see you?"

**4.** "Look upon the world as empty,
Mogharāja, ever mindful.
Having uprooted the theory of self,
you may thus cross over death.
That's how to look upon the world
so the King of Death won't see you."

## Atlas notes

The *Pārāyana* — "the way to the far shore," "the crossing-over" — closes the Sutta Nipāta and is, with the Aṭṭhakavagga that precedes it, among the very oldest strata of Buddhist poetry. Its frame is a journey: an aged brahmin, Bāvarī, sends sixteen of his students across India to put their questions to the Buddha, and the bulk of Book 5 is their sixteen brief catechisms. The three here, from different questioners, trace one movement — out of the world's current and across to the far shore. **Ajita** names the predicament: the world is "shrouded in ignorance," and its "streams" (the floods of craving and views that sweep beings along) run everywhere; what dams them is mindfulness, and what finally locks them out is wisdom — and the whole construction of "name and form" ceases "with the cessation of consciousness." **Upasīva** speaks for everyone who feels the difficulty directly: "I am not able to cross the great flood." The Buddha gives him a foothold (the meditative "perception of nothingness") and then, pressed about what becomes of the one who crosses, gives the canon's most haunting image: a freed sage is like "a flame tossed by a gust of wind" — it "comes to an end and cannot be reckoned"; "when all things have been eradicated, eradicated, too, are all ways of speech." And **Mogharāja** receives the most compressed instruction of all, the one later tradition prized above the rest: *look upon the world as empty, uproot the view of self, and you cross beyond death* — "so the King of Death won't see you."

## Context

The Pārāyana is Book 5 of the Sutta Nipāta, in the Khuddaka Nikāya. Like the Aṭṭhakavagga, it is quoted by name within the canon itself and was given an early canonical commentary (the *Cūḷaniddesa*), evidence of its antiquity and standing. Its sixteen questioners — Ajita, Tissametteyya, Puṇṇaka, Mettagū, Dhotaka, Upasīva, and the rest, ending with the old brahmin Piṅgiya — are remembered by name in the tradition. Mogharāja's answer ("look upon the world as empty") is one of the seeds of the later Buddhist philosophy of emptiness (*suññatā*), and Upasīva's "flame that cannot be reckoned" anticipates the canon's persistent refusal to say whether the liberated one "exists" or "does not exist" after death. The translation reproduced here is Bhikkhu Sujato's, dedicated to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

These are answers stripped to the bone — read them slowly, one exchange at a time. The recurring picture is water: a world of "streams" and a "great flood," and a self being carried along in it. The path is not to fight the current but to stop feeding it — mindfulness dams the streams, wisdom locks them out, craving's end dries the flood. Then sit with the strangeness of where this leads. Upasīva keeps asking the natural question — does the freed one still exist? — and the Buddha keeps declining to answer in those terms: the flame is not destroyed and not preserved, it is simply no longer findable, "beyond reckoning." Mogharāja's verse is the practical key to the whole book: it is the *view of self* that the King of Death tracks; look on the world as empty of self, and there is no one left for death to find.
