# Advice to Sigālaka (DN 31)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Long Discourses* (Dīgha Nikāya) 31, *Sigālovāda Sutta*, "Advice to Sigālaka." SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The full discourse, which details the duties of each relationship, is held in the Archive library under `long-discourses-sujato`.

Householder’s son, a noble disciple gives up four corrupt deeds, doesn’t do bad deeds on four grounds, and avoids six drains on wealth. When they’ve left these fourteen bad things behind they have the six directions covered.

*The six directions:*

These six directions should be recognized: parents as the east, tutors as the south, partner and children as the west, friends and colleagues as the north, bondservants and workers as beneath, and ascetics and brahmins as above.

*The closing verses:*

Parents are the east,
tutors the south,
wives and child the west,
friends and colleagues the north,
servants and workers below,
and ascetics and brahmins above.
By honoring these quarters
a householder does enough for their family.

## Atlas notes

The *Sigālovāda Sutta* is the canon's fullest statement of ethics for ordinary household life — so much so that the commentary calls it "the layperson's code of discipline." The Buddha meets a young man, Sigālaka, dutifully worshipping the six compass directions at his late father's request, without knowing why. Rather than dismiss the rite, the Buddha keeps the form and refills it: the six directions become the six classes of people to whom one has obligations, and "worship" becomes the practical discharge of duty. The teaching is reciprocal throughout — parents and children, teachers and students, spouses, friends, employers and workers, laypeople and ascetics each owe the other specific, named things. First, though, come the negatives to clear away: the four corrupt deeds (killing, stealing, lying, sexual misconduct), the four motives that drive bad action (desire, hate, fear, delusion), and the six ways wealth drains away (drink, late-night roaming, shows, gambling, bad friends, idleness).

## Context

The discourse belongs to the Dīgha Nikāya and is unusual in the canon for addressing itself wholly to lay life — to wealth, work, marriage, and friendship — rather than to monastic practice or meditation. It has been singled out by modern readers, including the Indian jurist B. R. Ambedkar, as evidence of an early Buddhist social ethic of mutual obligation that cut across the hierarchies of its time: note that the discourse gives employers duties toward workers and laypeople duties toward — not merely from — religious teachers.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the full discourse is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the "six directions" as the discourse's central move: a ritual of cosmic orientation is converted, without being mocked, into a chart of human relationships. Each direction is then unpacked into a short list of reciprocal duties — what a child owes parents *and* what parents owe the child, and so on around the compass. The ethic is concrete and symmetrical: not a set of ideals but a description of what honoring each relationship actually consists of.
