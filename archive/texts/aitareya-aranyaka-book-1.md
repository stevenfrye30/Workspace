# Aitareya-Aranyaka, Book 1

## Primary Text

F. Max Müller, trans., *The Upanishads, Part I*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. I (Oxford, 1879), public domain; the Aitareya-Aranyaka, First Aranyaka, opening of the first Adhyaya. The full translation is held in the Archive library under `upanishads-muller-part1`.

Now follows the Mahâvrata ceremony.

After having killed Vritra, Indra became great. When he became great, then there was the Mahâvrata (the great work). This is why the Mahâvrata ceremony is called Mahâvrata.

Some people say: "Let the priest make two recitations with the offering of the âgya (ghee) on that day," but the right thing is one.

He who desires proper food should use the hymn *agnim naro dîdhitibhih* (Rv. VII, 1, 1). Verily, Agni is the eater of food, and he thus becomes an eater of food.

## Atlas notes

Book 1 of the Aitareya-Aranyaka is the *ritual* portion of the text — a Brahmana-style exposition, not yet the philosophy of the Upanishad that follows. Its subject is the Mahavrata, the "great rite," the last-but-one day of the year-long Gavamayana Soma sacrifice. Across five chapters (Adhyayas) it works through the day's recitations (sastras) one by one — the Pra-uga, the Marutvatiya, the Nishkevalya, and the closing Agnimaruta — explaining which hymns are used, why, and what each secures.

Three ideas give the book its character. The first is correspondence: verses are counted and matched to the parts of the body and the divisions of the cosmos, so that a twenty-five-versed recitation answers to a twenty-five-part man and to Prajapati. The second is the recitation imagined as a great bird, its hymns forming right wing, left wing, trunk, and tail — a figure that anticipates the bird-shaped fire-altar of the later Agnicayana. The third is sound itself treated as sacred: "the sound Him is Brahman," and breath is identified with sound, prefiguring the speculation the Upanishad will take up directly.

## Context

The Aitareya-Aranyaka belongs to the Rigveda, to the Aitareya school that also produced the Aitareya-Brahmana. Modern scholarship places it in the late-Brahmana / early-Aranyaka period, roughly the eighth to sixth centuries BCE, contemporary with the older prose Upanishads. Müller's own note marks the seam within the work: "With the second Aranyaka the Upanishad begins. It comprises the second and third Aranyakas, and may be said to consist of three divisions, or three Upanishads. Their general title is Bahvrika-upanishad." Book 1, the First Aranyaka, is therefore the ritual prelude — the portion left outside that Upanishad.

The translation reproduced and described here is Müller's, from the first volume of the *Sacred Books of the East* (1879); it is held complete in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read Book 1 as ritual exposition, not as doctrine. Its sentences describe what the priest does and recites on the Mahavrata day, and its reasoning is the Brahmana's reasoning — by resemblance, etymology, and numerical fit rather than by argument. Kept in that register, the technical vocabulary (sastra, pragatha, nishkevalya, pra-uga) reads as a precise liturgical taxonomy rather than as obscurity. The philosophical pay-off comes in Books 2 and 3, where the same school turns from the rite to the self.
