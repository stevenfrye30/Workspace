# Wander Alone Like a Rhinoceros (Snp 1.3)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Anthology of Discourses* (Sutta Nipāta) 1.3, *Khaggavisāṇa Sutta*, "The Horned Rhino." SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `sutta-nipata-sujato`.

When you've laid down violence toward all creatures,
not harming even a single one,
don't wish for a child, let alone a companion:
live alone like a horned rhino.

Those with close relationships have affection,
following which this pain arises.
Seeing this danger born of affection,
live alone like a horned rhino.

As a wild deer loose in the forest
grazes wherever it wants,
a smart person looking for freedom would
live alone like a horned rhino.

If you find an alert companion,
an attentive friend to live happily together,
then, overcoming all adversities,
wander with them, joyful and mindful.

If you find no alert companion,
an attentive friend to live happily together,
then, like a king who flees his conquered realm,
wander alone like a tusker in the wilds.

They befriend you and serve you for their own sake;
these days it's hard to find friends lacking ulterior motive.
Impure folk cleverly profit themselves—
live alone like a horned rhino.

## Atlas notes

The *Khaggavisāṇa Sutta* — "the horn of the rhinoceros" — is among the oldest poems in the entire Buddhist canon, and one of the most striking. Through some forty verses it repeats a single refrain that has become famous in its own right: *live alone like a horned rhino.* The Indian rhinoceros bears a single horn, and so became a natural emblem of the one who walks unaccompanied. The poem is a sustained meditation on solitude as freedom, set against the entanglements that bind a life: affection carries within it the pain of loss; relationships, "like a spreading bamboo," catch and hold; and friends, it observes drily, often "serve you for their own sake." Against all of this it sets the ease of the one who walks alone, "as a wild deer grazes wherever it wants." Yet the poem is no counsel of coldness, and two of its verses make the qualification explicit and beautiful: *if you find an alert companion, an attentive friend, then wander with them, joyful and mindful* — and only *if you find no such friend* should you go "alone like a tusker in the wilds." Solitude is better than binding company, but not better than true company; and true company is precious and rare.

## Context

The discourse is the third text of the Sutta Nipāta, in the archaic *Uragavagga*, and is bound up with the early ideal of the *paccekabuddha*, the "solitary Buddha" who awakens without a teacher and does not found a community. Its antiquity is not merely literary: a version of this very poem survives in **Gāndhārī**, on birch-bark scrolls from the first century BCE or CE — among the oldest surviving Buddhist manuscripts in the world. These lines about going alone were thus among the very first Buddhist words ever committed to writing. Two of its verses (11–12) also appear in the Dhammapada (328–329).

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the refrain against its two great exceptions. The rhinoceros is the default, not an absolute: the poem prizes solitude as freedom from entanglement, then bows at once to genuine spiritual friendship wherever it can be found. Held together, the counsel is exact — do not cling, and do not settle for company that binds rather than frees; walk with the wise if you find them, and alone, gladly, if you do not. The single horn is not loneliness; it is a life not held hostage.
