# Aitareya-Aranyaka, Book 2 (Aitareya Upanishad)

## Primary Text

F. Max Müller, trans., *The Upanishads, Part I*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. I (Oxford, 1879), public domain; the Aitareya-Aranyaka, Second Aranyaka, fourth Adhyaya — the Aitareya Upanishad, the creation of food. The full translation is held in the Archive library under `upanishads-muller-part1`.

Those deities, Agni and the rest, after they had been sent forth, fell into this great ocean. Then he, the Self, besieged the person with hunger and thirst. The deities then spoke to him: "Allow us a place in which we may rest and eat food."

He led a cow towards them. They said: "This is not enough." He led a horse towards them. They said: "This is not enough." He led man towards them. Then they said: "Well done, indeed!"

He thought: "There are these worlds and the guardians of the worlds. Let me send forth food for them." He brooded over the water. From the water thus brooded on, matter (mûrti) was born. And that matter which was born, that verily was food.

## Atlas notes

Book 2 is the turn from rite to self. Its fourth, fifth, and sixth chapters form the *Aitareya Upanishad*, one of the principal Upanishads and one of the few on which both Shankara and Sayana wrote commentaries. The narrative reproduced above belongs to its creation account: the Self (Atman) sends forth the world and the world-guardian deities, then enters the human body as the faculties — and, finding hunger, sends forth food to sustain them. The Upanishad ends by asking what the Self truly is and answering with one of the tradition's "great sayings," *prajñānaṃ brahma* — "consciousness is Brahman." The whole movement is an argument that the maker of the world and the awareness within a person are one.

The book's earlier chapters (Adhyayas 1–3) are preparatory meditations on the *uktha*, the recited hymn, treated as breath and as Brahman — the bridge by which the ritual concern of Book 1 passes into the philosophy of the Upanishad.

## Context

The Aitareya Upanishad sits inside the Aitareya-Aranyaka of the Rigveda; Müller's editorial note marks exactly where the Upanishad starts: "With the second Aranyaka the Upanishad begins... Their general title is Bahvrika-upanishad." Counted on its own, the fourth Adhyaya of this Second Aranyaka becomes the first chapter of the Aitareya Upanishad. It belongs to the older prose Upanishads, roughly the sixth to fifth centuries BCE, and was carried in the same Rigvedic school as the Aitareya-Brahmana and the ritual First Aranyaka that precedes it here.

The translation reproduced and described here is Müller's (1879); it is held complete in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the creation story as a story about identity, not cosmology. The deities who "fall into the ocean" and beg for a resting place are the senses and faculties; the body they enter is the one each reader inhabits; the Self that projects them is not a distant creator but the awareness reading the sentence. The Upanishad's force is in collapsing that distance — which is why it closes not with a cosmogony but with the equation of consciousness and the absolute.
