# The Blind Men and the Elephant (Udāna 6.4)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Heartfelt Sayings* (Udāna) 6.4, *Tittha Sutta*. SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `udana-sujato`.

*Wanderers of various religions are quarreling at Sāvatthī, each insisting "this is the only truth" — that the cosmos is eternal, or not; that the soul and body are the same, or not; that a realized one exists after death, or not. The Buddha tells this story:*

Once upon a time, mendicants, right here in Sāvatthī there was a certain king. Then the king addressed a man, ‘Please, my man, gather all those blind from birth throughout Sāvatthī and bring them together in one place.’ … ‘Well then, lad, show them an elephant.’

To some of the blind people he showed the elephant's head … the ear … the tusk … the trunk … saying, ‘Here is the elephant.’ …

Then the king went up to the blind people and said, ‘Have you seen the elephant?’ The blind people who had been shown the elephant's head said, ‘Your Majesty, an elephant is like a pot.’ Those who had been shown the ear said, ‘An elephant is like a winnowing fan.’ Those who had been shown the tusk said, ‘An elephant is like a ploughshare.’ Those who had been shown the trunk said, ‘An elephant is like a draft-pole.’ … the foot … ‘a pillar.’

Saying, ‘Such is an elephant, not such! Such is not an elephant, such is!’ they punched each other with their fists. At that, the king was pleased.

In the same way, mendicants, the wanderers of other religions are blind and sightless. … That's why they are fighting, quarreling, and disputing, continually wounding each other with barbed words: ‘Such is Truth, such is not! Such is not Truth, such is!’

*Then the Buddha expressed this heartfelt sentiment:*

“Some ascetics and brahmins, it seems,
cling to these things.
Arguing, they dispute,
the folk who see just one part.”

## Atlas notes

This is the canonical source of one of the most widely travelled parables in the world. The Buddha tells it to explain why the rival teachers of his day fight so bitterly over the great speculative questions — whether the cosmos is eternal, whether the soul and body are one, whether an awakened one exists after death — each declaring his answer "the only truth." Long ago, he says, a king had the people born blind each shown a single part of an elephant: those who felt the head called it a pot, the ear a winnowing fan, the tusk a ploughshare, the trunk a draft-pole, the foot a pillar. Then, each certain that his part was the whole animal, they came to blows — "Such is an elephant, not such!" — while the king looked on, amused. The sectarians, the Buddha says, are exactly these blind men: each has hold of one real fragment of the truth and mistakes it for all of it, and so "the folk who see just one part" argue and wound one another over "Such is Truth, such is not." It is his diagnosis of dogmatic dispute — the views are not simply false (the blind men did touch a real elephant), but partial, seized one-sidedly and absolutized into a war.

## Context

The discourse belongs to the Udāna, the collection of the Buddha's "inspired utterances," and is set among the same unanswerable questions he declines elsewhere (see the poisoned arrow, MN 63). The parable far outgrew its origin: it passes into Jain and into Sufi tellings — Rumi and al-Ghazālī both use it — and into Hindu and modern Western versions, most famously John Godfrey Saxe's 1872 poem, becoming a near-universal emblem for the partiality of human knowledge. In many of those later versions the elephant stands for God or for ultimate reality; in the Buddha's own telling it points more sharply at the futility of the metaphysical views he set aside.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

The parable cuts two ways, and the Buddhist edge is the sharper one. It is tempting to read it as gentle relativism — everyone holds part of the truth, so be tolerant. But the Buddha's stress falls on the *clinging*: the blind men err not in touching a real part but in seizing it as the whole and fighting for it. And tellingly, his remedy is not to assemble all the parts into one grand view — the very questions at issue are ones he refuses to answer at all — but to loosen the grip that turns a fragment into a creed and a creed into a quarrel. Read beside the poisoned arrow, the lesson is consistent: some things are best not absolutized in the first place.
