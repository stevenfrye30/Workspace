# The Chariot: Is There a Self? (Milindapañha)

## Primary Text

John Kelly, trans., *Milindapañha* (The Questions of Milinda) 3.1.1, "Discussion of the Designation." SuttaCentral. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `milindapanha-kelly`.

“How is the reverend one known, what is your name, venerable sir?”

“I am called Nāgasena, your majesty … Though parents make such names as Nāgasena, or Sūrasena … Nāgasena is only a concept, a designation, a label, an appellation, a mere name; no person actually obtains here.”

*The king objects: if no person is found, who then practices, who acts, who reaps the fruit of deeds? He presses Nāgasena — are the hairs of the head Nāgasena? the nails, teeth, skin, flesh, bones …? "Certainly not." Then Nāgasena turns the question around:*

“… your majesty, how did you come here, on foot or in a vehicle?” “I did not come on foot, venerable sir, I came in a chariot.” “If, your majesty, you came in a chariot, tell me about a chariot. Is the carriage pole the chariot?” “Certainly not, venerable sir.” “Is the axle the chariot? … Are the wheels …? Is the body of the chariot …? Is the flag-staff … the yoke … the reins … the goad the chariot?” “Certainly not, venerable sir.”

“Then, your majesty, are the carriage pole, axle, wheels, body of the chariot, flag-staff, yoke, reins, and goad the chariot?” “Certainly not, venerable sir.” “Then, your majesty, is something apart from the carriage pole, axle, wheels … the chariot?” “Certainly not, venerable sir.”

“Though, your majesty, I am asking you repeatedly, I do not see a chariot. Is a chariot only the sound ‘chariot’? … Then, what here is a chariot? You speak a falsehood, your majesty, there is no chariot.”

*The king relents:*

“I do not speak falsely, venerable sir … because of the carriage pole, the axle, the wheels, the body of the chariot, the flag-staff, there exists a ‘chariot’, as a concept, a designation, a label, an appellation, a mere name.”

“Good, your majesty, you know a chariot. In the same way, your majesty, because of the head hairs, the body hairs … and because of form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, consciousness, there exists ‘Nāgasena’, as a concept, a designation, a label, an appellation, a mere name; however in the absolute sense there obtains no person here.”

## Atlas notes

The *Milindapañha*, the "Questions of Milinda," records a series of dialogues between a Buddhist sage, Nāgasena, and a king named Milinda — Menander I, the Indo-Greek ruler who reigned over the northwest of the subcontinent around 150 BCE, in the long aftermath of Alexander. This, the most famous exchange in the book, is the classic Buddhist demonstration of *anattā*, not-self. Nāgasena begins provocatively: his own name, he says, is "only a designation, a mere name; no person actually obtains here." The king reasonably protests — if there is no person, who keeps the precepts, who acts, who is reborn? Nāgasena answers not by arguing but by examining the king's own chariot. Is the pole the chariot? the axle? the wheels? Is the chariot all of them together, or something separate from all of them? To each, the king must say no — and is brought to admit that "chariot" is simply a convenient word for the assembled parts, with no chariot-essence over and above them. And just so, Nāgasena concludes, "Nāgasena" is a designation for an assembled body and the five aggregates: real as a convention, empty of any separate, abiding self. The image proved so exact that it became the standard teaching picture for not-self across the Buddhist world — the self is like a chariot, perfectly real to use and to name, but not a thing-in-itself.

## Context

The Milindapañha took shape around the first century BCE to the second CE and is paracanonical — not in the canon proper, but revered enough to be included in the Burmese Tipiṭaka. It is a rare and precious record of the meeting of Greek and Indian thought in the Hellenistic Buddhist kingdoms of Gandhāra. The chariot analogy itself is older than the text: it restates a canonical verse spoken by the nun Vajirā (Saṃyutta Nikāya 5.10), "just as, with an assembly of parts, the word 'chariot' is used, so, when the aggregates are present, there's the convention 'a being.'"

The translation reproduced above is John Kelly's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

The simile cuts precisely between two errors. It does not say there is no Nāgasena — the king really did converse with someone, and deeds really do bear fruit. It says there is no Nāgasena as a separate essence standing behind the parts. The middle position — real as a process and a name, empty as a substance — is exactly what not-self means. And notice the method: Nāgasena wins not by asserting a doctrine but by leading the king to discover, in his own everyday word "chariot," that he never actually believed in hidden essences to begin with.
