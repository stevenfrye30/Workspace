# The Simile of the Raft (MN 22)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Middle Discourses* (Majjhima Nikāya) 22, *Alagaddūpama Sutta*, "The Simile of the Cobra" — the simile of the raft. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The full discourse is held in the Archive library under `middle-discourses-sujato`.

“Mendicants, I will teach you a simile of the teaching as a raft: for crossing over, not for holding on. Listen and apply your mind well, I will speak.” “Yes, sir,” they replied. The Buddha said this:

“Suppose there was a person traveling along the road. They’d see a large deluge, whose near shore was dubious and perilous, while the far shore was a sanctuary free of peril. But there was no ferryboat or bridge for crossing over. They’d think, ‘Why don’t I gather grass, sticks, branches, and leaves and make a raft? Riding on the raft, and paddling with my hands and feet, I can safely reach the far shore.’ And so they’d do exactly that.

And when they’d crossed over to the far shore, they’d think, ‘This raft has been very helpful to me. Riding on the raft, and paddling with my hands and feet, I have safely crossed over to the far shore. Why don’t I hoist it on my head or pick it up on my shoulder and go wherever I want?’ What do you think, mendicants? Would that person be doing what should be done with that raft?” “No, sir.”

“And what, mendicants, should that person do with the raft? When they’d crossed over they should think, ‘This raft has been very helpful to me. … Why don’t I beach it on dry land or set it adrift on the water and go wherever I want?’ That’s what that person should do with the raft.

In the same way, I have taught a simile of the teaching as a raft: for crossing over, not for holding on. By understanding the simile of the raft, you will even give up the teachings, let alone what is not the teachings.”

## Atlas notes

The simile of the raft is the canon's classic statement of non-attachment applied to the teaching itself. A traveler stranded on the dangerous near shore of a flood builds a raft from whatever is at hand, paddles across, and reaches safety. What then? Only a fool would lift the raft onto his head and lug it overland out of gratitude; the sensible traveler leaves it at the water's edge and walks on. The Dhamma, the Buddha says, is exactly such a raft — indispensable for the crossing, pointless to carry once across. The startling conclusion follows: "you will even give up the teachings, let alone what is not the teachings." Right views are still to be relinquished at the far shore; how much more, then, the wrong ones. In its home discourse this comes right after a complementary warning — the simile of the water-snake — that grasping the teaching *badly*, like seizing a snake by the tail, can wound the one who holds it.

## Context

The raft appears in the *Alagaddūpama Sutta* ("The Simile of the Cobra," or water-snake) of the Majjhima Nikāya, a discourse aimed at a monk who had twisted the teaching into a license. The two similes there work as a pair: learn the Dhamma rightly, as one grasps a snake safely behind the head; and hold it lightly, as one uses a raft and lets it go. The raft has become one of the most cited images in Buddhism, invoked wherever the tradition warns against turning its own doctrines into objects of clinging or identity.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the full discourse is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Hold both halves of the image. The raft must first be *built and used* — the teaching is not optional for the crossing, and the discourse is no charter for discarding it before one has reached the far shore. The letting-go applies at the destination: what was a vehicle is not to become a burden, and not to become an identity. Read together with the snake simile that precedes it, the lesson is balance — grasp the Dhamma well enough to cross on it, lightly enough to step off it.
