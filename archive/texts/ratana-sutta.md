# The Discourse on the Jewels (Snp 2.1)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Anthology of Discourses* (Sutta Nipāta) 2.1, *Ratana Sutta*, "Gems" — the opening appeal and the three central "jewel" verses with the closing. SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The full discourse is held in the Archive library under `sutta-nipata-sujato`.

Whatever beings have gathered here,
on the ground or in the sky:
may beings all be of happy heart,
and listen carefully to what is said.

So pay heed, all you beings,
have love for humankind,
who day and night bring offerings;
please protect them diligently.

There’s no wealth in this life or the next,
no sublime gem in the heavens,
that equals the Realized One.
This sublime gem is in the Buddha:
by this truth, may you be well!

Ending, dispassion, the undying, the sublime,
attained by the Sakyan Sage immersed in samādhi;
there is nothing equal to that Dhamma.
This sublime gem is in the Dhamma:
by this truth, may you be well!

The eight individual persons praised by the good
are the four pairs of the Holy One’s disciples;
they are worthy of religious donations,
what’s given to them is very fruitful.
This sublime gem is in the Saṅgha:
by this truth, may you be well!

Whatever beings have gathered here,
on the ground or in the sky:
the Realized One is honored by gods and humans!
We bow to the Saṅgha! May you be safe!

## Atlas notes

The *Ratana Sutta* is one of the great protective recitations (*parittā*) of the Theravada world, chanted for safety, healing, and blessing. Its form is the "act of truth" (*saccakiriya*): each central verse states something true about one of the Three Jewels — the Buddha, the Dhamma, the Saṅgha — and then invokes that very truth as a power for good: "by this truth, may you be well!" The Buddha is the gem no heavenly treasure equals; the Dhamma is the deathless peace reached in deep meditation, beyond compare; the Saṅgha is the community of the truly accomplished, "the four pairs, the eight persons," supremely worth honoring. The discourse opens by asking the unseen beings of a place to be kind to the humans who make them offerings, and closes with those beings bowing to the Three Jewels in turn.

## Context

By tradition the Ratana Sutta was first spoken at Vesālī, a city beset at once by famine, disease, and fear of spirits; the Buddha taught these verses, and Venerable Ānanda walked the city walls reciting them through the night, sprinkling water, until the troubles lifted. Whatever the history of that frame, it fixed the discourse's use: it is recited to ward off danger and to bless new houses, ventures, and gatherings, alongside its companions the Maṅgala and Mettā suttas. It belongs to the Sutta Nipāta, among the oldest strata of the canon.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the full discourse is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Notice where the protective power is located. The Ratana Sutta is not a spell; its "magic" is the act of truth — the old Indian idea that a true statement, sincerely made, has force in the world. Each verse therefore *does* something only because what it asserts is the case: the blessing rides on the reality of the Buddha, the Dhamma, and the Saṅgha, not on the words as incantation. Read that way, reciting it is an exercise in calling to mind, precisely, what is worth taking refuge in.
