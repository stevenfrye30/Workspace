# The Going Forth and the Striving (Sutta Nipāta 3.1–3.2)

## Primary Text

**Selected verses** — from two poems in Book 3 (the Great Chapter) of the Sutta Nipāta. Pabbajjā: verses 1–2 and 16–20 of 20; Padhāna: verses 1–3, 6–8, 12–16, 19, and 22–25 of 25. Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Anthology of Discourses* (Sutta Nipāta) 3.1 (Going Forth) and 3.2 (Striving). SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). Verse numbers are Sujato's; gaps in the numbering and an ellipsis (…) mark omitted verses. The Pali root text is held alongside in the Archive library under `sutta-nipata-sujato`.

**Sutta Nipāta 3.1 — Going Forth (Pabbajjā).**

**1.** "I shall extol going forth
with the example of the Clear-eyed One,
the course of inquiry that led to
his choice to go forth.

**2.** 'This life at home is cramped,
a realm of dirt.'
'The life of one gone forth is like an open space.'
Seeing this, he went forth."

…

**16.** "You are young, just a youth,
a lad in the prime of life.
You are endowed with beauty and stature,
like an aristocrat of good birth

**17.** in glory at the army's head,
surrounded by a troop of elephants.
I offer you pleasures—enjoy them!
But please tell me of your birth.'

**18.** 'Up north, O King,
on the slopes of the Himalayas,
full of wealth and strength, lies the land
of one whose abode is among the Kosalans.

**19.** Their clan is named for the Sun,
they are Sakyans by birth.
I have gone forth from that family—
I do not yearn for sensual pleasure.

**20.** Seeing the danger in sensual pleasures,
seeing renunciation as sanctuary,
I shall go on to strive;
that is where my mind delights.'"

**Sutta Nipāta 3.2 — The Striving (Padhāna).**

**1.** "During my time of resolute striving
back on the bank of the Nerañjara River,
I was meditating very hard
for the sake of sanctuary from the yoke.

**2.** Namucī approached,
speaking words of kindness:
'You're thin, discolored,
on the verge of death.

**3.** Death has a thousand parts of you,
one fraction is left to life.
Live, worthy sir! Life is better!
Living, you can make merits.'"

…

**6.** When Māra had spoken in this way,
the Buddha said this:
"O Wicked One, kinsman of the negligent,
you're here for your own purpose.

**7.** I have no need for
the slightest bit of merit.
Those with need for merit
are fit for Māra to address.

**8.** I have faith and energy too,
and wisdom is found in me.
When I am so resolute,
why do you beg me to live?"

…

**12.** Sensual pleasures are your first army,
the second is called discontent,
hunger and thirst are the third,
and the fourth is said to be craving.

**13.** Your fifth is dullness and drowsiness,
the sixth is said to be cowardice,
your seventh is doubt,
contempt and obstinacy are your eighth.

**14.** Profit, praise, and honor,
and misbegotten fame;
the extolling of oneself
while scorning others.

**15.** This is your army, Namucī,
the strike force of the Dark One.
Only a hero can defeat it,
but in victory there lies bliss.

**16.** Let me gird myself—
curse my life!
I'd rather die in battle
than live on in defeat.

…

**19.** That army of yours has never been beaten
by the world with all its gods.
Yet I shall smash it with wisdom,
like an unfired pot with a stone."

…

**22.** "For seven years I followed
step by step behind the Blessed One.
I found no vulnerability
in the mindful Awakened One.

**23.** A crow once circled a stone
that looked like a lump of fat.
'Perhaps I'll find something tender,' it thought,
'perhaps there's something tasty.'

**24.** But finding nothing tasty,
the crow left that place.
Like the crow that pecked the stone,
I leave Gotama disappointed."

**25.** So stricken with sorrow
that his harp dropped from his armpit,
that spirit, downcast,
vanished right there.

## Atlas notes

These two poems are among the oldest things the canon preserves about the Buddha's own life — not the embellished biography of later centuries, but a spare, early-verse memory of the two decisive moments before the awakening. The **Pabbajjā**, "Going Forth," opens with the renunciation in a single famous contrast: *"This life at home is cramped, a realm of dirt; the life of one gone forth is like an open space."* It then tells of the newly homeless Sakyan begging for alms in Rājagaha, where King Bimbisāra, struck by his bearing, rides out and offers him wealth, pleasures, and a place at the head of his army — and the young man declines it all, naming his lineage ("their clan is named for the Sun, they are Sakyans by birth") and his resolve: *"seeing the danger in sensual pleasures, seeing renunciation as sanctuary, I shall go on to strive."* That last word leads straight into the **Padhāna**, "The Striving" — the canon's version of the temptation in the wilderness. As the Bodhisatta meditates to the point of death on the bank of the Nerañjarā, Māra (here called Namucī, "the Tempter") comes with soft, reasonable words: *you are wasting away; live, make merit, the path of striving is too hard.* The Bodhisatta answers that he has no use for merit, only faith, energy, and wisdom, and then names the real adversary — the **armies of Māra**, which are not demons but states of mind: sensual desire, discontent, hunger, craving, dullness, cowardice, doubt, conceit. *"Only a hero can defeat it, but in victory there lies bliss… I'd rather die in battle than live on in defeat."* He vows to "smash it with wisdom, like an unfired pot with a stone," and Māra, who shadowed him for seven years and "found no vulnerability," departs like a crow that pecked a stone hoping for fat — his very harp slipping from under his arm.

## Context

Book 3 of the Sutta Nipāta, the "Great Chapter" (*Mahāvagga*), gathers longer narrative and doctrinal poems, and the Pabbajjā and Padhāna stand at its head as a matched pair on renunciation and the struggle to awaken. They are early enough that the later legendary apparatus is almost wholly absent: there is no palace luxury, no chariot rides past the four sights, only a man who finds the household life confining and leaves it. The Padhāna's "armies of Māra" became one of the most influential images in Buddhism, and its psychologizing of temptation — Māra's forces are named as ordinary mental hindrances — is characteristic of the tradition's refusal to externalize evil. The translation reproduced here is Bhikkhu Sujato's, dedicated to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the two together as one arc: the going-out and the going-through. The Pabbajjā's power is its understatement — a king offers everything and is simply, courteously refused, because the young man has already seen that pleasures end in danger and freedom feels "like an open space." Then notice what kind of story the Padhāna is. Māra does not roar; he counsels *moderation*, and his case is sensible — you're killing yourself, live and do good. The temptation that nearly works is the reasonable one. And when the Bodhisatta lists Māra's armies, he is naming his own mind: desire, discontent, sloth, doubt, conceit. The battle is internal, the enemy is recognized rather than slain, and the victory is not conquest but the steadiness that gives the Tempter nothing to grip — the crow finds no fat on the stone, and leaves.
