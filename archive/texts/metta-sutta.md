# The Discourse on Love (Snp 1.8)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Anthology of Discourses* (Sutta Nipāta) 1.8, *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta*, "The Discourse on Love." SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text (Mahāsaṅgīti edition) is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `sutta-nipata-sujato`.

Those who are skilled in the meaning of scripture
should practice as follows to realize the state of peace.
Let them be capable and upright, very upright,
easy to speak to, gentle and humble;

content and unburdensome,
unbusied, living lightly,
alert, with senses calmed,
courteous, not fawning on families.

Let them not do the slightest thing
that others who are wise would blame.
May they be happy and safe!
May all beings be happy!

Whatever living creatures there are
with not a one left out—
frail or firm, long or large,
medium, small, tiny or round,

seen or unseen,
living far or near,
those who have been born and those about to be born—
may all beings be happy!

Let none deceive another,
nor look down on anyone anywhere.
Though provoked or aggrieved,
let them not wish pain on each other.

Even as a mother would protect with her life
her child, her only child,
so too for all creatures
unfold a boundless heart.

With love for the whole world,
unfold a boundless heart:
above, below, all round,
unconstricted, without enmity or foe.

When standing, walking, sitting,
or lying down while yet unweary,
keep this ever in mind;
for this, they say, is a divine meditation in this life.

Avoiding harmful views,
virtuous, accomplished in insight,
with desire for sensual pleasures dispelled,
they never return to a womb again.

## Atlas notes

The *Mettā Sutta* is the canon's central statement on loving-kindness (*mettā*), and one of the most recited texts in the Buddhist world. It moves in three stages. It opens as a list of virtues — uprightness, gentleness, contentment, freedom from busyness — the character of one fit to realize peace. It then turns to the practice itself: the wish "may all beings be happy," extended deliberately across every category the mind might use to exclude (frail or firm, seen or unseen, near or far, already born or yet to be born), so that no being is left out. Its most famous image gives the feeling its measure: as a mother would guard her only child with her life, so one is to "unfold a boundless heart" toward all that lives — above, below, and all around, without enmity. The closing verse names the fruit: held in mind in every posture, this is "a divine meditation in this life."

## Context

The discourse belongs to the Sutta Nipāta, an early verse anthology in the Khuddaka Nikāya whose language is among the oldest in the canon. It is grouped with the canon's "protective" recitations (*parittā*) and is chanted for blessing and for calming fear; the traditional frame-story has the Buddha teaching it to monks troubled by tree-spirits in a forest, that they might dwell there in safety through goodwill rather than dread. Its Pali title, *Karaṇīya Mettā*, comes from its first word, *karaṇīya*, "what should be done."

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's, released to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read it as instructions, not sentiment. The middle stanzas are a method: the wish for happiness is pushed past each boundary the mind draws — size, visibility, distance, even existence — until the exclusions run out. The mother-and-child image is precise about intensity, not partiality: it names how fierce the care should be, then immediately universalizes it. And the final stanzas insist the practice is not a mood but a posture to be kept "when standing, walking, sitting, or lying down" — continuous, not occasional.
