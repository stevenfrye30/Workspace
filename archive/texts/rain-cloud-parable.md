# The Rain Cloud and the Plants (Lotus Sūtra, Ch. 5)

## Primary Text

H. Kern, trans., *The Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka, or The Lotus of the True Law*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XXI (Oxford, 1884), translated from the Sanskrit; public domain. From Chapter 5, "On Plants." The full text is held in the Archive library under `lotus-sutra-kern`.

*The image:*

It is a case, Kâsyapa, similar to that of a great cloud big with rain, coming up in this wide universe over all grasses, shrubs, herbs, trees of various species and kind … a cloud covering the wide universe to pour down its rain everywhere and at the same time. Then … the grasses, shrubs, herbs, and wild trees in this universe … will each, according to its faculty and power, suck the humid element from the water emitted by that great cloud, and by that water which, all of one essence, has been abundantly poured down by the cloud, they will each, according to its germ, acquire a regular development, growth, shooting up, and bigness … Rooted in one and the same soil, all those families of plants and germs are drenched and vivified by water of one essence throughout.

*Its meaning:*

Even as the great cloud … pours out the same water and recreates by it all grasses, shrubs, herbs, and trees; even as all these … according to their faculty, power, and strength, suck in the water and thereby attain the full development assigned to their kind; in like manner, Kâsyapa, is the law preached by the Tathâgata … of one and the same essence, that is to say, the essence of it is deliverance, the final aim being absence of passion, annihilation, knowledge of the all-knowing. … I am he, Kâsyapa, who, knowing the law which is of but one essence, viz. the essence of deliverance … do not on a sudden reveal to all the knowledge of the all-knowing, since I pay regard to the dispositions of all beings.

*From the verses that follow:*

I recreate the whole world like a cloud shedding its water without distinction; I have the same feelings for respectable people as for the low; for moral persons as for the immoral. … I preach the law to the inferior as well as to persons of superior understanding and extraordinary faculties; inaccessible to weariness, I spread in season the rain of the law.

## Atlas notes

The Parable of the Medicinal Herbs is the third of the Lotus Sūtra's great parables of "skillful means," and it shifts the emphasis from the teacher's method to the teaching's reach. The Buddha's Dharma, the parable says, is a single rain cloud that rises over the whole world and lets fall one and the same water — "the essence of deliverance" — on every living thing without exception or favoritism: "the same feelings for respectable people as for the low, for moral persons as for the immoral." But the plants that drink it are not the same. Grasses, shrubs, herbs, and towering trees each absorb the one water "according to its faculty and power" and grow to the size assigned to its kind — and the parable then names them: the small plants are those who seek their own liberation, the middling are the solitary realizers, the great are the bodhisattvas. So the One Vehicle of Chapter 3 and the patient father of Chapter 4 are joined here by a third figure: a teaching that is offered wholly and equally, and received in as many measures as there are beings to receive it.

## Context

The parable belongs to Chapter 5 of the Lotus Sūtra, where the Buddha confirms the understanding the senior disciples expressed in the lost-son parable of Chapter 4. With the burning house (Ch. 3) and the lost son (Ch. 4), it completes the sūtra's central sequence on how a single goal is reached by many means and at many speeds. The public-domain English is Hendrik Kern's 1884 translation from the Sanskrit; in the East Asian tradition the chapter is known as the "Parable of the Medicinal Herbs."

The translation reproduced above is Kern's; the full text is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Hold the equality and the difference together. The rain is one and falls without distinction — the teaching is not rationed by merit, caste, or virtue. Yet the plants differ, and each can only take in as much as its nature allows. The parable refuses both errors: it is not that the Buddha teaches different things to different people (the water is one essence), nor that all receive the same (each grows to its own kind). The realism is in the reception, the generosity in the offer — and only the Buddha, the parable adds, truly sees which plant is which.
