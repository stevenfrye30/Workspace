# The Honey-Cake (MN 18)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Middle Discourses* (Majjhima Nikāya) 18, *Madhupiṇḍika Sutta*, "The Honey-Cake." SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `middle-discourses-sujato`.

*The Buddha's terse statement:*

“Mendicant, judgments driven by proliferating perceptions beset a person. If they don't find anything worth approving, welcoming, or getting attached to in the source from which these arise, just this is the end of the underlying tendencies to desire, aversion, views, doubt, conceit, the desire for continued existence, and ignorance. This is the end of taking up the rod and the sword, the end of quarrels, arguments, and disputes, of accusations, backbiting, and lies.”

*The elder Mahā Kaccāna, asked to unpack it, traces the chain:*

Eye consciousness arises dependent on the eye and sights. The meeting of the three is contact. Contact is a requirement for feeling. What you feel, you perceive. What you perceive, you think about. What you think about, you proliferate. What you proliferate is the source from which judgments driven by proliferating perceptions beset a person. This occurs with respect to sights known by the eye in the past, future, and present.

*Ānanda gives the discourse its name:*

“Sir, suppose a person who was weak with hunger was to obtain a honey-cake. Wherever they taste it, they would enjoy a sweet, delectable flavor. In the same way, wherever a sincere, capable mendicant might examine with wisdom the meaning of this exposition of the teaching they would only gain joy and clarity. … what is the name of this exposition of the teaching?”

“Well then, Ānanda, you may remember this exposition of the teaching as ‘The Honey-Cake Discourse’.”

## Atlas notes

This discourse holds one of the most psychologically acute ideas in all of Buddhism: *papañca*, which Sujato renders "proliferation" — the mind's compulsive tendency to elaborate, to build whole worlds of judgment, story, and self out of a single flicker of raw experience. The Buddha states it in a sentence so compressed that the monks cannot follow it, and ask the elder Mahā Kaccāna — famed for unfolding in detail what the Buddha gives in brief — to explain. His exposition is so clear and nourishing that Ānanda compares it to a starving man's first taste of a honey-cake, sweet wherever you bite, and the discourse takes its name from the image. Kaccāna's chain is the heart of it: from eye and sight arises consciousness, then contact, then feeling — and then the mind takes over: *what you feel, you perceive; what you perceive, you think about; what you think about, you proliferate* — until that proliferation becomes "the source from which judgments beset a person." And those besieging judgments are not harmless: they are the soil of desire and aversion, of conceit and fixed views, and finally of "taking up the rod and the sword," of quarrels and lies. In a single chain the discourse links the most intimate event — a half-noticed perception — to the most public catastrophe, war. The cure is placed at the source: find nothing there worth grasping, and the whole proliferating mass, with all the conflict it breeds, simply has nowhere to grow.

## Context

The *Madhupiṇḍika Sutta* belongs to the Majjhima Nikāya, and is a showcase for Mahā Kaccāna, whom the Buddha named foremost among those who analyze in full what he states in brief. Its key term, *papañca* — and the phrase *papañca-saññā-saṅkhā*, "reckonings born of proliferating perception" — became one of the most studied concepts in Buddhist psychology, a precise name for the mind's self-entangling activity.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Watch where the chain changes character. Up to "feeling," the process runs by itself — eye meets sight, contact happens, a feeling arises; there is nothing to be done about that. The turn comes at *perceive → think → proliferate*, where the mind begins to spin, and a bare event becomes "me, mine, for, against." The discourse locates suffering not in the senses but in this elaboration, and locates freedom not in shutting the senses down but in not finding, at the source, anything worth seizing on. It is the same hinge as dependent origination — feeling tipping into craving — seen here from the side of thought.
