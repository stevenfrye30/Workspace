# Aṅgulimāla, the Murderer Who Stopped (MN 86)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Middle Discourses* (Majjhima Nikāya) 86, *Aṅgulimāla Sutta*. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `middle-discourses-sujato`.

Now at that time in the realm of King Pasenadi of Kosala there was a bandit named Aṅgulimāla. He was violent, bloody-handed, a hardened killer, merciless to living beings. … He was constantly murdering humans, and he wore their fingers as a necklace.

*The Buddha walks into the forest where Aṅgulimāla preys, and Aṅgulimāla gives chase:*

But the Buddha used his psychic power to will that Aṅgulimāla could not catch up with him no matter how hard he tried, even though the Buddha kept walking at a normal speed. … He stood still and said, “Stop, stop, ascetic!”

“I’ve stopped, Aṅgulimāla—now you stop.”

*Aṅgulimāla, puzzled that a man still walking should say he has stopped, asks in verse:*

“While walking, ascetic, you say ‘I’ve stopped.’ And I have stopped, but you tell me I’ve not. I’m asking you this, ascetic: how is it you’ve stopped and I have not?”

“Aṅgulimāla, I have forever stopped— I’ve laid aside violence towards all creatures. But you can’t stop yourself from harming living creatures; that’s why I’ve stopped, but you have not.”

“Oh, at long last a renowned great seer, an ascetic has followed me into this deep wood. Now that I’ve heard your verse on Dhamma, I shall live without evil.” With these words, the bandit hurled his sword and weapons down a cliff into an abyss. He venerated the Holy One’s feet, and asked him for the going forth right away. … And with that he became a monk.

*Later, now a monk, Aṅgulimāla comes upon a woman in obstructed labor, and the Buddha tells him to make this declaration of truth:*

“Ever since I was born in the noble birth, sister, I don’t recall having intentionally taken the life of a living creature. By this truth, may both you and your baby be safe.” Then that woman was safe, and so was her baby.

## Atlas notes

Aṅgulimāla — the name means "finger-garland" — was a serial killer who wore his victims' severed fingers around his neck and had laid waste to the kingdom of Kosala. The Buddha walks alone into the forest where he hunts. Aṅgulimāla gives chase, but for the first time in his life cannot catch his quarry, though the Buddha only strolls; and when he shouts "Stop!", the answer is the riddle that turns him: *"I have stopped, Aṅgulimāla; now you stop."* The Buddha has stopped *forever* — he has laid down all violence — while the killer, for all his running, has not. Hearing it, Aṅgulimāla throws his weapons into the abyss and ordains on the spot. But the discourse refuses an easy ending. As a monk he walks for alms and is pelted with stones and clods, returning with a cracked head and torn robe — and the Buddha tells him to endure it, for he is experiencing here and now the ripening of deeds that would otherwise have cost him ages in hell. The story's last image is its tenderest: the former killer, told to help a woman whose childbirth has gone wrong, can find no true thing to swear by until he reaches back only as far as his *ordination* — and by that truth, mother and child are saved.

## Context

The *Aṅgulimāla Sutta* belongs to the Majjhima Nikāya. Aṅgulimāla became one of the venerated arhats of the early community, and his verses are preserved in the Theragāthā. His "act of truth" over the labouring woman is recited to this day across the Theravada world as the *Aṅgulimāla paritta*, a blessing for safe childbirth — a remarkable afterlife for the words of a reformed murderer. The story has long been read as the tradition's clearest teaching on redemption and on the unflinching logic of karma.

## Reading note

Hold the two turns together. The first is "stopping": the Buddha's stillness is moral, not physical — the cessation of harm — while the killer's frantic motion is the restlessness of a mind still bound; that is why a walking man can have stopped and a standing man not. The second is the hard half most retellings drop: ordination does not erase what Aṅgulimāla did. He is stoned in the streets and bears it as the harvest of his own deeds. Redemption here is real but not cheap — it transforms who he is without rewriting what he did, and its proof is that he can stand in the harm coming back to him without striking out again.
