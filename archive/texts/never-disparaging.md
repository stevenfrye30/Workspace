# The Bodhisattva Never-Disparaging (Lotus Sūtra, Ch. 19)

## Primary Text

H. Kern, trans., *The Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka, or The Lotus of the True Law*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XXI (Oxford, 1884), translated from the Sanskrit; public domain. From Chapter 19, "Sadâparibhûta." The full text is held in the Archive library under `lotus-sutra-kern`.

At the time succeeding the complete extinction of that Gina, when the decay of the true law was far advanced, there was a monk, a Bodhisattva, called by the name of Sadâparibhûta.

Other monks and nuns who did not believe but in what they saw, he would approach and say: I never am to contemn you, for you observe the course leading to supreme enlightenment.

It was his wont always to utter those words, which brought him but abuse and taunts from their part. At the time when his death was impending he heard this Sûtra.

The sage, then, did not expire; he resolved upon a very long life, and promulgated this Sûtra under the rule of that leader.

And those many persons who only acknowledged the evidence of sensual perception were by him brought to full ripeness for enlightenment. Then, disappearing from that place, he propitiated thousands of kotis of Buddhas.

Owing to the successive good actions performed by him, and to his constantly promulgating this Sûtra, that son of Gina reached enlightenment. That Bodhisattva then is myself, Sâkyamuni.

## Atlas notes

The bodhisattva's name, Sadāparibhūta, carries a deliberate double meaning: "Never-Disparaging" and "Always-Disparaged." His whole practice was a single gesture. He would approach anyone at all — monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen, the devout and the hostile alike — bow, and say: *I do not despise you, for you are all destined to become Buddhas.* For this he was mocked, cursed, and pelted with sticks and stones; chased off, he would call the same words from a safe distance. He never wavered, and he never claimed anything for himself — only proclaimed what others were becoming. The chapter's turn is the revelation that closes it: that despised, persistent bodhisattva was the Buddha himself, Śākyamuni, in a former life — and the very people who had abused him were, after long ages, the disciples now seated before him. The story is the Lotus Sūtra's most radical application of its own teaching that all beings will reach Buddhahood: if that is true, then the only adequate response to any person, including one's tormentor, is reverence. Sadāparibhūta did not preach the doctrine of universal Buddhahood; he practiced it, by seeing the future Buddha in every face.

## Context

Chapter 19 of Kern's translation (Chapter 20 in Kumārajīva's Chinese) is among the best loved in the East Asian Lotus tradition, and central to Nichiren Buddhism, which takes Sadāparibhūta's bow as a model: reverence for all beings as Buddhas-to-be, enacted bodily. It belongs to the cluster of chapters in which the Buddha's past lives and the deep history of the teaching are unfolded. The public-domain English is Hendrik Kern's 1884 translation from the Sanskrit. (Kern romanizes *Jina*, "the Victor," as "Gina.")

The translation reproduced above is Kern's; the full text is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Let the two halves of the name teach each other. He "never disparaged" precisely because he could see what others were becoming; he was "always disparaged" precisely because they could not. His one sentence is a complete practice — to treat every person, even one who strikes you, as a Buddha in the making — and the chapter insists it is not naïve sentiment but the hardest and highest discipline, since it was the path by which a Buddha himself was formed. The final reveal turns the story from a curiosity into an instruction: this is offered as something to do.
