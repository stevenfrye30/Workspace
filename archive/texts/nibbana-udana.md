# The Unconditioned (Udāna 8.1, 8.3)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Heartfelt Sayings* (Udāna) 8.1 and 8.3. SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `udana-sujato`.

*Udāna 8.1:*

“There is, mendicants, that dimension where there is no earth, no water, no fire, no wind; no dimension of infinite space, no dimension of infinite consciousness, no dimension of nothingness, no dimension of neither perception nor non-perception; no this world, no other world, no moon or sun. There, mendicants, I say there is no coming or going or remaining or passing away or reappearing. It is not established, does not proceed, and has no support. Just this is the end of suffering.”

*Udāna 8.3:*

“There is, mendicants, freedom from rebirth, freedom from what has been produced, made, and conditioned. If there were no freedom from rebirth, freedom from what has been produced, made, and conditioned, then you would find no escape here from rebirth, from what has been produced, made, and conditioned. But since there is freedom from rebirth, freedom from what has been produced, made, and conditioned, an escape is found from rebirth, from what has been produced, made, and conditioned.”

## Atlas notes

These two "inspired utterances" are as close as the Pali canon comes to describing *nibbāna* — extinguishment, the goal of the whole path — directly. And they do it almost entirely by taking things away. The first withdraws every coordinate by which experience is normally located: not the four elements (earth, water, fire, wind), not the subtle formless states of deep meditation, not this world or any other, not even sun or moon; and then the verbs of existence go too — no coming, no going, no remaining, no passing away, no reappearing; "not established, does not proceed, has no support." What is left is given only one name: "the end of suffering." The second states the entire rationale of the path in a single conditional. There is an unborn, unmade, unconditioned — and *if there were not*, there could be no escape from the born, the made, the conditioned; but because there is, an escape is found. Held together, the two passages guard the most delicate point in Buddhist thought. Nibbāna is not annihilation — it is spoken of as real, as "there," as an actual freedom and escape. And it is not a thing, place, or state to be grasped — every positive description is deliberately revoked. The Buddha describes it the only way it can be honestly described: by removing all that it is not, and letting the silence stand.

## Context

These verses come from the eighth and final chapter of the Udāna, the collection of the Buddha's spontaneous utterances, which gathers his sayings on nibbāna alongside the events of his last days. They are among the most quoted and most argued-over lines in all of Buddhism — the touchstone of every attempt to say what nibbāna "is," and the canon's clearest instance of the *via negativa*, the way of negation. The formula of 8.3, "the unborn, unmade, unconditioned" (*ajāta, abhūta, akata, asaṅkhata*), is one of the tradition's most famous phrases.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Resist filling the silence. The passages are built to close both of the mind's escape routes at once. To the thought "then it is simply nothing," they answer: no — there is an escape, a freedom, it is *there*. To the thought "then it is some hidden thing, a place or a heaven," they answer: no — not established, no support, every locating word denied. The teaching lives precisely in that double refusal. Read the negations slowly, letting each one shut a door, until what is left is not a new concept to hold but the cessation the entire path was for.
