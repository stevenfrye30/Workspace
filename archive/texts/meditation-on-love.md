# The Radiance of Love (Itivuttaka 27)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *So It Was Said* (Itivuttaka) 27, "The Meditation on Love." SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `itivuttaka-sujato`.

“Mendicants, of all the grounds for making worldly merit, none are worth a sixteenth part of the heart's release by love. Surpassing them, the heart's release by love shines and glows and radiates.

It's like how the radiance of all the stars is not worth a sixteenth part of the moon's radiance. … It's like the last month of the rainy season, in autumn, when the heavens are sunny and cloudless. And as the sun is rising to the firmament, having dispelled all the darkness of space, it shines and glows and radiates. … It's like how, at the crack of dawn, the Morning Star shines and glows and radiates. In the same way, of all the grounds for making worldly merit, none are worth a sixteenth part of the heart's release by love.”

*The verses:*

A mindful one who develops
limitless love
weakens the fetters,
seeing the ending of attachments.

Loving just one creature with a hateless heart
makes you a good person.
Sympathetic for all creatures,
a noble one creates abundant merit.

The royal potentates conquered this land
and traveled around sponsoring sacrifices—
horse sacrifice, human sacrifice,
the sacrifices of the 'casting of the yoke-pin', the 'royal soma drinking', and the 'unimpeded'.

These are not worth a sixteenth part
of the mind developed with love.

## Atlas notes

This short text of the Itivuttaka makes the boldest claim in the canon for loving-kindness (*mettā*). Of all the grounds for making "worldly merit" — every generous and meritorious act there is — the Buddha says not one is worth a *sixteenth part* of "the heart's release by love," the mind set free through boundless goodwill. He presses it with a cascade of light: as the moon outshines all the stars, as the cloudless autumn sun dispels every shadow, as the morning star blazes at dawn, so the loving heart "shines and glows and radiates," surpassing every other good. Then the verses push further, in two directions at once. Inward: cultivating limitless love "weakens the fetters" — *mettā* is not merely a kindness but a path, loosening the bonds of attachment toward liberation. And outward, with a sharp edge: the great kings, it says, conquered the earth and sponsored the most prestigious rites of the age — the horse sacrifice, the human sacrifice, the soma rituals — and all of them together "are not worth a sixteenth part of the mind developed with love." In a single stroke the text lifts the cultivation of a loving heart above the entire apparatus of sacrificial religion. Love, not the altar, is the supreme offering.

## Context

The Itivuttaka, "the as-it-was-saids," is a collection of short prose-and-verse sayings in the Khuddaka Nikāya. This passage is the canonical basis for the exalted standing of *mettā* among the four "divine abodes" (*brahmavihāra*), and one of several places the Buddha quietly dethrones Vedic sacrifice in favour of an ethic of the heart. It is the natural companion to the *Mettā Sutta* (Snp 1.8): where that text teaches *how* to cultivate love, this one declares *what it is worth*.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Hold the two claims together. Love is exalted here not as the nicest of the virtues but as *cetovimutti* — a "release of the heart," a genuine liberation that weakens the very fetters the path exists to cut. The goodwill the Buddha praises, then, is not a passing warm feeling but a trained, boundless state that does real spiritual work. And the comparison to the sacrifices is no aside: in its setting it is a deliberate revaluation — the merit a king buys with rivers of sacrificial blood is outshone, sixteen times over, by a single mind developed in love.
