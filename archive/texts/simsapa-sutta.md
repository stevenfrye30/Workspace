# In a Rosewood Forest (SN 56.31)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Linked Discourses* (Saṃyutta Nikāya) 56.31, *Sīsapāvana Sutta*, "In a Rosewood Forest." SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text (Mahāsaṅgīti edition) is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `linked-discourses-sujato`.

At one time the Buddha was staying near Kosambī in a rosewood grove. Then the Buddha picked up a few rosewood leaves in his hand and addressed the mendicants:

“What do you think, mendicants? Which is more: the few leaves in my hand, or those in the grove above?”

“Sir, the few leaves in your hand are a tiny amount. There are far more leaves in the grove above.”

“In the same way, there is much more that I have directly known but have not explained to you. What I have explained is a tiny amount.

And why haven’t I explained it? Because it’s not beneficial or relevant to the fundamentals of the spiritual life. It doesn’t lead to disillusionment, dispassion, cessation, peace, insight, awakening, and extinguishment. That’s why I haven’t explained it.

And what have I explained? I have explained: ‘This is suffering’ … ‘This is the origin of suffering’ … ‘This is the cessation of suffering’ … ‘This is the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering’.

And why have I explained this? Because it’s beneficial and relevant to the fundamentals of the spiritual life. It leads to disillusionment, dispassion, cessation, peace, insight, awakening, and extinguishment. That’s why I’ve explained it.

That’s why you should practice meditation …”

## Atlas notes

This short discourse states the Buddha's principle of selection: he teaches by relevance, not by completeness. Holding up a few leaves against the whole forest above, he makes the proportion vivid — what he has directly known is the forest; what he has taught is the handful. The criterion for the difference is purely practical: he leaves unspoken everything that does not *lead* anywhere — to disillusionment, dispassion, peace, awakening — and teaches only what does, which turns out to be the four noble truths. The discourse is the canon's clearest answer to the metaphysical questions it elsewhere sets aside: their omission is not ignorance but economy. The teaching is medicine, dispensed for a result, and a teacher with a forest of knowledge gives only the dose that heals.

## Context

The *Sīsapāvana Sutta* belongs to the *Saccasaṃyutta*, the connected discourses on the truths, the same chapter of the Saṃyutta Nikāya that opens with the first sermon. It is frequently paired with the *Cūḷamālukya Sutta* (MN 63, the simile of the poisoned arrow), which makes the same point through a different image: the wounded man who refuses treatment until idle questions are answered. Together they form the canon's statement of what is sometimes called the Buddha's pragmatism — the deliberate restriction of the teaching to what bears on the end of suffering.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the leaves as a claim about *purpose*, not a boast about hidden knowledge. The point is not that the Buddha is withholding secrets but that he has a single aim — freedom from suffering — and measures everything against it. Questions that do not move toward that aim, however interesting, are left in the forest. The discourse invites the reader to apply the same test to their own attention: of all that could be known, what actually leads somewhere worth going?
