# Proverbs, Chapter 1

Chapter 1 of the Book of Proverbs is the opening of the principal wisdom book of the Hebrew Bible. After a seven-verse opening that names Solomon, states the book's purpose, and gives the book's motto, the chapter divides into two long speeches. The first is a father's address to his son, warning him not to be drawn in by violent companions. The second is the figure of Wisdom herself, personified as a woman, calling aloud in the public streets and at the city gates, summoning the simple to listen and naming what comes to those who refuse. The chapter sets the book's principal voices: the household teacher, and Wisdom in public speech.

## Primary Text

Proverbs, chapter 1, verses 1–33 (complete). *The Holy Scriptures According to the Masoretic Text* (Jewish Publication Society, 1917); public domain. The text follows the JPS 1917 edition (Wikisource transcription), preserving the print’s poetic-line capitalization and rendering the divine name “LORD”; quotation marks are given as the 1917 print’s curly directional marks, as on this archive’s other Tanakh pages. Wording verified against Sefaria and Mechon-Mamre; one Wikisource transcription slip at 1:20 (“street”) is corrected to the JPS reading “streets,” per both other witnesses.

**1.** The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel,

**2.** To know wisdom and instruction; To comprehend the words of understanding;

**3.** To receive the discipline of wisdom, Justice, and right, and equity;

**4.** To give prudence to the simple, To the young man knowledge and discretion;

**5.** That the wise man may hear, and increase in learning, And the man of understanding may attain unto wise counsels;

**6.** To understand a proverb, and a figure; The words of the wise, and their dark sayings.

**7.** The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge; But the foolish despise wisdom and discipline.

**8.** Hear, my son, the instruction of thy father, And forsake not the teaching of thy mother;

**9.** For they shall be a chaplet of grace unto thy head, And chains about thy neck.

**10.** My son, if sinners entice thee, Consent thou not.

**11.** If they say: ‘Come with us, Let us lie in wait for blood, Let us lurk for the innocent without cause;

**12.** Let us swallow them up alive as the grave, And whole, as those that go down into the pit;

**13.** We shall find all precious substance, We shall fill our houses with spoil;

**14.** Cast in thy lot among us; Let us all have one purse’—

**15.** My son, walk not thou in the way with them, Restrain thy foot from their path;

**16.** For their feet run to evil, And they make haste to shed blood.

**17.** For in vain the net is spread In the eyes of any bird;

**18.** And these lie in wait for their own blood, They lurk for their own lives.

**19.** So are the ways of every one that is greedy of gain; It taketh away the life of the owners thereof.

**20.** Wisdom crieth aloud in the streets, She uttereth her voice in the broad places;

**21.** She calleth at the head of the noisy streets, At the entrances of the gates, in the city, she uttereth her words:

**22.** ‘How long, ye thoughtless, will ye love thoughtlessness? And how long will scorners delight them in scorning, And fools hate knowledge?

**23.** Turn you at my reproof; Behold, I will pour out my spirit unto you, I will make known my words unto you.

**24.** Because I have called, and ye refused, I have stretched out my hand, and no man attended,

**25.** But ye have set at nought all my counsel, And would none of my reproof;

**26.** I also, in your calamity, will laugh, I will mock when your dread cometh;

**27.** When your dread cometh as a storm, And your calamity cometh on as a whirlwind; When trouble and distress come upon you.

**28.** Then will they call me, but I will not answer, They will seek me earnestly, but they shall not find me.

**29.** For that they hated knowledge, And did not choose the fear of the LORD;

**30.** They would none of my counsel, They despised all my reproof.

**31.** Therefore shall they eat of the fruit of their own way, And be filled with their own devices.

**32.** For the waywardness of the thoughtless shall slay them, And the confidence of fools shall destroy them.

**33.** But whoso hearkeneth unto me shall dwell securely, And shall be quiet without fear of evil.’

## Context

Proverbs is one of the three principal wisdom books of the Hebrew Bible, alongside Job and Ecclesiastes. Of the three, it is the most direct and the most extensively instructional: thirty-one chapters of teaching, much of it in the two-line aphoristic form that has come to define the genre in English. The book carries Solomon's name in its opening verse, and tradition long associated it with him; modern scholarship treats the Solomonic attribution as a literary frame and dates the compilation across a long span — older proverbial material gathered, edited, and prefaced over several centuries — with the present book reaching its form during the Persian and early Hellenistic period (roughly fifth to third centuries BCE).

The book is composed of several distinct collections, marked off by their own headings (Proverbs 1:1, 10:1, 22:17, 24:23, 25:1, 30:1, 31:1). Chapters 1–9 form the first collection: a sustained prologue of long instructional speeches, distinct in form from the short paired aphorisms that fill chapters 10–29. Chapter 1 stands at the head of this prologue. After the seven-verse opening that names Solomon, states the book's purpose, and gives the book's motto ("the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge," 1:7), the chapter contains two complete speeches: a father's instruction to his son (1:8–19), and the public address of personified Wisdom (1:20–33). The two voices recur, in alternation, through the rest of the prologue.

## Reading note

Two habits help. First, the chapter moves between two distinct voices in two distinct settings. Verses 8–19 are the father's voice, addressed to his son in the household, warning against being drawn in by men who lay in wait for blood. Verses 20–33 shift to Wisdom personified as a woman — no longer in the household, but outside in the streets and at the city gates, calling aloud to anyone who will listen. Reading the chapter with the change of setting visible — household instruction in the first speech, public address in the second — recovers a structural feature of the book's prologue.

Second, both speeches turn on what their hearers do or fail to do. The father instructs and warns; Wisdom invites, then names the consequences for those who have refused her — "they shall call upon me, but I will not answer; they shall seek me earnestly, but they shall not find me." Reading the closing verses with attention to the shift from invitation to consequence, rather than smoothing the two into a single moral exhortation, keeps the form the chapter has.
