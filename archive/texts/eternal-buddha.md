# The Eternal Buddha (Lotus Sūtra, Ch. 15)

## Primary Text

H. Kern, trans., *The Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka, or The Lotus of the True Law*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XXI (Oxford, 1884), translated from the Sanskrit; public domain. From Chapter 15, "Duration of Life of the Tathâgata" — the parable of the good physician and the verses that follow. The full text is held in the Archive library under `lotus-sutra-kern`.

*The parable of the good physician:*

There is some physician, learned, intelligent, prudent, clever in allaying all sorts of diseases. That man has many sons … The physician once being abroad, all his children incur a disease from poison or venom. … Their father, the physician, comes home … prepares a great remedy, having the required colour, smell, and taste … and gives it as a potion to his sons. … Those amongst the children that have right notions … quickly take it, and in consequence of it are soon totally delivered from their disease. But the sons who have perverted notions … do not take the remedy offered …

Then the physician reflects thus: … Therefore will I by some able device induce these sons to take this remedy. … Having thus admonished them, he skilfully betakes himself to another part of the country and lets his sick sons know that he has departed life. They are extremely sorry and bewail him extremely: So then he is dead, our father and protector … now are we left without a protector. … plunged in sorrow, by which their perverted notions make room for right notions. They acknowledge that remedy … to have the required colour, smell, and taste, so that they instantly take it, and by taking it are delivered from their evil. Then, on knowing that these sons are delivered from evil, the physician shows himself again.

… Would any one charge that physician with falsehood on account of his using that device? No, certainly not, Lord. … In the same manner, young men of good family, I have arrived at supreme, perfect enlightenment since an immense, incalculable number of hundred thousands of myriads of kotis of Æons, but from time to time I display such able devices to the creatures, with the view of educating them, without there being in that respect any falsehood on my part.

*From the verses:*

I show the place of extinction, I reveal to (all) beings a device to educate them, albeit I do not become extinct at the time, and in this very place continue preaching the law.

… I was not completely extinct at that time; it was but a device of mine, monks; repeatedly am I born in the world of the living.

Feel no doubt concerning it, O sages, and leave off all uncertainty: the word I here pronounce is really true; my word is never false.

## Atlas notes

This is the Lotus Sūtra's deepest and most startling teaching, and the hinge on which much of East Asian Buddhism turns. The Buddha reveals that the story everyone knows — the prince who left home, awakened under the tree, taught for forty-five years, and died at Kusinārā — is not the whole truth. In reality he attained enlightenment "an incalculable number of aeons ago" and has never ceased teaching since; what looks like a single human lifetime is one appearance of a Buddha who is, in effect, ever-present. Why, then, does he seem to die? Because beings grow complacent when they believe the Buddha is always at hand; his passing is a compassionate shock, a *device*. The parable of the good physician makes it exact: a skilled doctor cannot persuade his poison-stricken, stubborn children to take the antidote, so he goes away and sends word that he has died — and grief brings them to their senses, and they take the cure, and then he returns. The Buddha's death, the Lotus says, is that feigned death: not an end, but the last and greatest of his skillful means.

## Context

Chapter 15 (in some numberings 16), "The Lifespan of the Tathāgata," is the doctrinal climax of the Lotus Sūtra. It is the textual foundation for the East Asian conception of the eternal or cosmic Buddha — the Buddha not as a man who lived and died but as an ever-present reality — which underlies the *dharmakāya* doctrine, the devotion of the Pure Land schools to Amitābha, and the Nichiren focus on the "original Buddha." It is the natural counterpart, within the Archive, to the Pāli account of the Buddha's death in the *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (DN 16): the same event, read from opposite ends of the Buddhist world. The public-domain English is Hendrik Kern's 1884 translation.

The translation reproduced above is Kern's; the full text is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read this beside the Pāli death-scene, and the two great vehicles come into focus. In the *Mahāparinibbāna* the Buddha truly dies, and his last words — "conditions fall apart; persist with diligence" — draw their force from that finality: rely on yourself and the teaching, because even a Buddha passes. In the Lotus the Buddha never really dies, and the lesson is the reverse: do not presume on his absence, for he is always here, and his "death" was love. Neither cancels the other; together they mark the distance the tradition travelled. The recurring question — was the physician lying? — is the Lotus's steady answer to the worry that all this talk of "devices" makes the Buddha a deceiver: a compassionate appearance, aimed at healing, is not a lie.
