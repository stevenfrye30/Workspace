# The Diamond Sūtra — Müller's Sanskrit (Vajracchedikā)

## Primary Text

**Selected passages** (§§ I–V and XXXII, drawn from the sūtra's 32 sections; Müller's scholarly footnotes are omitted). F. Max Müller, trans., "The Vagrakkhedikā or Diamond-Cutter," in *Buddhist Mahâyâna Texts*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XLIX (Oxford, the Clarendon Press, 1894); public domain; sourced from the clean Internet Sacred Text Archive HTML of SBE XLIX (retrieved via the Internet Archive Wayback Machine). Müller's English is reproduced verbatim, including his parenthetical glosses. His italic-letter transliteration is rendered in standard form: the diacritical letters as macron-and-accent forms (ā, ī, ū, ñ, ṇ, etc.), and his italic consonants in their standard values — so Müller's *Vagrakkhedikā*, *Pragñā-pāramitā*, and *Geta* appear here as Vajracchedikā, Prajñā-pāramitā, and Jeta — while his digraph spellings such as "Bhikshu" are kept. One obvious OCR slip has been corrected: in § I, "round hira" → "round him." An ellipsis (…) marks where Müller's text is shortened — at the start of an excerpt that begins partway into its section, or within a passage.

§ I. Thus it was heard by me: At one time Bhagavat (the blessed Buddha) dwelt in Śrāvastī, in the grove of Jeta, in the garden of Anāthapiṇḍada, together with a large company of Bhikshus (mendicants), viz. with 1250 Bhikshus, with many noble-minded Bodhisattvas. Then Bhagavat having in the forenoon put on his undergarment, and having taken his bowl and cloak, entered the great city of Śrāvastī to collect alms. … he put away his bowl and cloak, washed his feet, and sat down on the seat intended for him, crossing his legs, holding his body upright, and turning his reflection upon himself. Then many Bhikshus approached to where Bhagavat was, saluted his feet with their heads, turned three times round him to the right, and sat down on one side.

§ II. … 'How then, O Bhagavat, should the son or the daughter of a good family, after having entered on the path of the Bodhisattvas, behave, how should he advance, and how should he restrain his thoughts?' After the venerable Subhūti had thus spoken, Bhagavat said to him: 'Well said, well said, Subhūti! So it is, Subhūti, so it is, as you say. …'

§ III. Then the Bhagavat thus spoke to him: 'Any one, O Subhūti, who has entered here on the path of the Bodhisattvas must thus frame his thought: As many beings as there are in this world of beings, comprehended under the term of beings … all these must be delivered by me in the perfect world of Nirvāṇa. And yet, after I have thus delivered immeasurable beings, not one single being has been delivered. And why? If, O Subhūti, a Bodhisattva had any idea of (belief in) a being, he could not be called a Bodhisattva … no one is to be called a Bodhisattva, for whom there should exist the idea of a being, the idea of a living being, or the idea of a person.'

§ IV. 'And again, O Subhūti, a gift should not be given by a Bodhisattva, while he believes in objects; a gift should not be given by him, while he believes in anything; a gift should not be given by him, while he believes in form; a gift should not be given by him, while he believes in the special qualities of sound, smell, taste, and touch. For thus, O Subhūti, should a gift be given by a noble-minded Bodhisattva, that he should not believe even in the idea of cause. … the measure of the stock of merit of a Bodhisattva, who gives a gift without believing in anything, is not easy to learn.'

§ V. 'Now, what do you think, O Subhūti, should a Tathāgata be seen (known) by the possession of signs?' Subhūti said: 'Not indeed, O Bhagavat, a Tathāgata is not to be seen (known) by the possession of signs. And why? Because what has been preached by the Tathāgata as the possession of signs, that is indeed the possession of no-signs.' After this, Bhagavat spoke thus to the venerable Subhūti: 'Wherever there is, O Subhūti, the possession of signs, there is falsehood; wherever there is no possession of signs, there is no falsehood. Hence the Tathāgata is to be seen (known) from no-signs as signs.'

§ XXXII. 'And, O Subhūti, if a noble-minded Bodhisattva were to fill immeasurable and innumerable spheres of worlds with the seven treasures, and give them as a gift to holy and fully enlightened Tathāgatas; and if a son or a daughter of a good family, after taking from this treatise of the Law, this Prajñā-pāramitā, one Gāthā of four lines only, should learn it, repeat it, understand it, and fully explain it to others, then the latter would on the strength of this produce a larger stock of merit, immeasurable and innumerable. And how should he explain it? As in the sky:

Stars, darkness, a lamp, a phantom, dew, a bubble.  
A dream, a flash of lightning, and a cloud—thus we should look upon the world (all that was made).

Thus he should explain; therefore it is said: He should explain.' Thus spoke the Bhagavat enraptured. The elder Subhūti, and the friars, nuns, the faithful laymen and women, and the Bodhisattvas also, and the whole world of gods, men, evil spirits and fairies, praised the preaching of the Bhagavat.

Thus is finished the Diamond-cutter, the blessed Prajñā-pāramitā.

## Atlas notes

This is the *Diamond Sūtra* in its Sanskrit dress — Müller's 1894 translation of the *Vajracchedikā*, "the (wisdom) that cuts like a diamond." It carries the same teaching as the Chinese-recension version held elsewhere in the archive (Gemmell's translation from Kumārajīva), but it is worth reading the two side by side, because they descend from different lines of transmission and the wording diverges in revealing ways. The doctrine is one continuous act of cutting. The bodhisattva resolves that "all these must be delivered by me in the perfect world of Nirvāṇa," and in the same breath sees that "not one single being has been delivered" — because for a true bodhisattva "there should exist no idea of a being, the idea of a living being, or the idea of a person." Generosity follows the same rule: "a gift should not be given by a Bodhisattva while he believes in anything." And the Buddha himself cannot be pinned to his marks — "wherever there is the possession of signs, there is falsehood." Müller's closing gāthā is the most famous lines in the sūtra, and notice that his Sanskrit yields *nine* images, not the six of the common Chinese verse: "Stars, darkness, a lamp, a phantom, dew, a bubble. A dream, a flash of lightning, and a cloud — thus we should look upon the world." The characteristic logic throughout is negation that loops back: a thing is preached as no-thing, and is *therefore* truly called the thing — language used to pry the mind off its own categories.

## Context

The *Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā* took shape in the early centuries CE within the Perfection of Wisdom literature. It survives both in Sanskrit manuscripts and in several Chinese translations, of which Kumārajīva's (c. 402 CE) became the most influential in East Asia. The archive holds two public-domain witnesses: William Gemmell's 1912 English from Kumārajīva's Chinese (the entry **The Diamond Sūtra**), and — here — F. Max Müller's 1894 English directly from the Sanskrit, in his *Sacred Books of the East* volume of Mahāyāna texts. Comparing them shows how recension and translator shape a text: section divisions, the names of Subhūti's interlocutor formulas, and above all the closing simile-list differ between the Sanskrit and the Chinese line. The sūtra also holds a unique place in the history of the book: the dated copy (868 CE) recovered from the sealed cave-library at Dunhuang is the world's earliest complete printed book bearing a date, now in the British Library.

## Reading note

If you have read the Gemmell version, read this one slowly against it: the teaching is identical, the music is not. Müller's Sanskrit keeps the relentless dialectical drumbeat — "preached as X, therefore no-X, therefore called X" — more visibly than the smoother Chinese-based English, and that repetition is the point, not a translator's tic. Do not try to resolve the paradoxes into ordinary sense; feel where they press. "Deliver all beings, yet no being is delivered" is not a contradiction to be solved but a way of acting with total commitment while refusing to freeze self, other, or achievement into fixed things. The closing gāthā is the gentlest door: hold the whole shimmering world as stars at dawn, a lamp, dew, a bubble, a dream — and the grip relaxes on its own.
