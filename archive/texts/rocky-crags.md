# These Rocky Crags Delight Me (Theragāthā 18.1)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Verses of the Senior Monks* (Theragāthā) 18.1, the verses of Mahā Kassapa. SuttaCentral, 2019. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `theragatha-sujato`.

Glistening, they look like blue stormclouds,
with waters cool and streams so clear,
and covered all in ladybugs:
these rocky crags delight me!

Like the peak of a blue stormcloud,
or like a fine bungalow, lovely,
echoing with the trumpeting of elephants:
these rocky crags delight me!

The rain comes down on the lovely flats,
in the mountains frequented by seers.
Echoing with the cries of peacocks,
these rocky crags delight me!

It's enough for me, who loves absorption,
to remain resolute.
It's enough for me,
a resolute monk who wants what's best.

It's enough for me,
a resolute monk who loves comfort.
It's enough for me,
resolute and unaffected, loving meditation.

Empty of householders,
frequented by herds of deer,
full of flocks of many different birds,
these rocky crags delight me!

The water's clear and the rocks are broad,
monkeys and deer are all around;
festooned with dewy moss,
these rocky crags delight me!

## Atlas notes

The Theragāthā, the "Verses of the Senior Monks," is the companion volume to the Therīgāthā — the poems of the first generation of awakened monks, the men's counterpart to the nuns' anthology. This long poem belongs to Mahā Kassapa, one of the Buddha's foremost disciples, the great ascetic who, after the Buddha's death, convened the council that preserved the teaching. Its most celebrated passage is a sustained hymn to the wild places where he meditates, each verse turning on the refrain *these rocky crags delight me.* Hills glisten like blue stormclouds; streams run cool and clear; slopes echo with the trumpeting of elephants and the cries of peacocks; valleys lie "empty of householders" and "frequented by herds of deer." It is among the earliest and loveliest nature poetry in world literature — and what makes it unmistakably Buddhist is the turn it takes at its center. The delight is not in scenery for its own sake but in what the wilderness affords: solitude, and the conditions for meditation. "It's enough for me, who loves absorption, to remain resolute." The crags delight the elder precisely because they are empty of the human bustle that entangles and full of the silence in which a mind can settle. Beauty and renunciation, usually set against each other, are here one and the same.

## Context

The Theragāthā belongs to the Khuddaka Nikāya and is among the oldest strata of the canon; with the Therīgāthā it forms the personal, near-autobiographical voice of the first awakened generation. Mahā Kassapa's is one of its longest poems. The "forest-dweller" (*āraññaka*) ideal it celebrates — the practitioner who leaves the village for the wilds — is one of the most ancient currents in Buddhism, and this poem is its classic expression.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Notice how the landscape and the meditation are braided, not merely placed side by side. The verses never say "the forest is beautiful, but I came here to practice"; they say the forest's emptiness of people and fullness of wild life is exactly what makes it delightful to one who "loves absorption." Read the refrain as a contemplative's, not a tourist's: the crags delight because they ask nothing of him and offer everything the path needs — quiet, space, and the absence of the crowd.
