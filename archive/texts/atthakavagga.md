# The Aṭṭhakavagga (Sutta Nipāta, Book 4)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Anthology of Discourses* (Sutta Nipāta), the *Aṭṭhakavagga*, "Chapter of Octads" (Book 4): suttas 4.5, 4.8, 4.9, 4.12, and 4.13. SuttaCentral, 2021. **Sutta Nipāta 4.5 — "Eight on the Ultimate."**

**1.** If, maintaining that theirs is the "ultimate" view,
a personage makes it out to be highest in the world;
then they declare all others are "lesser";
that's why they're not beyond disputes.

**3.** Those who are skilled say that, too, is a knot,
relying on which people see others as lesser.
That's why a mendicant ought not rely
on what's seen, heard, or thought, or on precepts and vows.

**5.** What was picked up has been set down and is not grasped again;
they form no dependency even on notions.
They follow no side among the factions,
and believe in no view at all.

**7.** For them not even the tiniest idea is formulated here
regarding what is seen, heard, or thought.
That brahmin does not grasp any view—
how could anyone in this world judge them?

**8.** They don't make things up or promote them,
and don't subscribe to any of the doctrines.
The brahmin has no need to be led by precept or vow;
gone to the far shore, one such does not return.

**Sutta Nipāta 4.8 — "With Pasūra."**

**5.** When these disputes come up among ascetics,
they get excited or dejected.
Seeing this, refrain from contention,
for the only purpose is praise and profit.

**9.** When someone disputes about a view they've adopted,
saying, "This is the only truth,"
say to them, "Here you'll have no adversary
when a dispute has come up."

**10.** … Who is there to argue with you, Pasūra,
among those who grasp nothing here as the highest?

**Sutta Nipāta 4.9 — "With Māgaṇḍiya."**

**3.** "After judging among the teachings,"
said the Buddha to Māgaṇḍiya,
"none have been adopted thinking, 'I assert this.'
Seeing views without adopting any,
searching, I saw inner peace."

**13.** There are no ties for one detached from ideas;
there are no delusions for one freed by wisdom.
But those who have adopted ideas and views
wander the world causing conflict.

**Sutta Nipāta 4.12 — "The Shorter Discourse on Arrayed for Battle."**

**7.** "The truth is one, there is no second,
understanding which folk would not dispute.
But those ascetics each boast of different truths;
that's why they don't say the same thing."

**17.** Standing in judgment, measuring by their own standard,
they dispute with the world more and more.
But a personage who has given up all judgments
creates no conflict in the world.

**Sutta Nipāta 4.13 — "The Longer Discourse on Arrayed for Battle."**

**2.** That is a small thing, insufficient for peace,
these two fruits of dispute, I say.
Seeing this, one ought not dispute,
looking for sanctuary in the land of no dispute.

**18.** Having untied the knots here in the world,
the sage takes no side among disputes.
Peaceful among the peaceless, equanimous,
they don't grasp when others grasp.

**20.** … With burden put down, the sage is released:
not formulating, not abstaining, not longing.

## Atlas notes

The *Aṭṭhakavagga*, the "Chapter of Octads," is one of the oldest layers of the entire Buddhist canon — spare, pre-systematic verse that names almost none of the later doctrinal lists and instead circles a single nerve: the danger of *views*. Its target is not thinking but *clinging* to a position as "the ultimate" and making oneself superior by it. "Here alone is purity," the disputants say — and that very claim, the poems insist, is "a knot." The sage of these verses "follows no side among the factions, and believes in no view at all"; having "judged among the teachings," they have adopted none of them as a banner. From this comes a peculiar, hard-won peace that is at once inward and social: where the proud "dispute with the world more and more," the one who has "given up all judgments creates no conflict in the world." The two "Arrays for Battle" (4.12–4.13) lay bare the logic of sectarian quarrel — each school certain it alone is right, each calling the others fools on the very grounds by which it praises itself — and answer it not with a better argument but with a stepping-aside: "the sage takes no side among disputes… Peaceful among the peaceless." The chapter's last word is release: "with burden put down… not formulating, not abstaining, not longing."

## Context

The *Aṭṭhakavagga* and the *Pārāyana* (Books 4 and 5 of the Sutta Nipāta, in the Khuddaka Nikāya) are widely held to be among the earliest Buddhist texts — quite possibly older than the prose discourses of the four main Nikāyas, and circulating as named collections while the canon was still forming. The *Aṭṭhakavagga* is quoted by title elsewhere in the canon, and it received its own canonical commentary, the *Mahāniddesa*, very early — a sign of how anciently it was treasured. Its concern with the sage who has "gone beyond" and grasps no view links it to the Sutta Nipāta's other early poems (the *Uraga* and *Khaggavisāṇa* discourses) and anticipates, in seed, the later Mahāyāna critique of fixed views. The translation here is Bhikkhu Sujato's, dedicated to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Do not mistake this for anti-intellectualism. The sage of the Octads has "judged among the teachings" — discernment is intact; what is given up is *identification*, the move by which a view becomes "mine" and its holder becomes "right" and others "fools." Read the dispute poems (4.8, 4.12, 4.13) as a mirror held up to the mind's hunger to win: notice how the craving for "praise and profit" rides inside even high doctrinal debate. And notice that the peace offered is not retreat into a private certainty — that would just be one more view — but a lightness that lets others grasp without grasping back: "Peaceful among the peaceless, equanimous, they don't grasp when others grasp."
