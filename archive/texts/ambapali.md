# Ambapālī on Age (Therīgāthā 13.1)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Verses of the Senior Nuns* (Therīgāthā) 13.1, the verses of Ambapālī. SuttaCentral, 2019. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `therigatha-sujato`.

My hair was as black as bees,
graced with curly tips;
now old, it has become like hemp bark—
the word of the truthful one is confirmed.

With plaits of black and ribbons of gold,
it was so pretty, adorned with braids;
now old, my head's gone bald—
the word of the truthful one is confirmed.

My eyebrows used to look so nice,
like crescents painted by an artist;
now old, they droop with wrinkles—
the word of the truthful one is confirmed.

My ear-lobes were so pretty,
like lovingly crafted bracelets;
now old, they droop with wrinkles—
the word of the truthful one is confirmed.

My hands used to be so pretty,
adorned with lovely golden rings;
now old, they're like red radishes—
the word of the truthful one is confirmed.

Both my feet used to be so pretty,
plump as if with cotton-wool;
now old, they're cracked and wrinkly—
the word of the truthful one is confirmed.

This bag of bones once was such,
but now it's withered, home to so much pain;
like a house in decay with plaster crumbling—
the word of the truthful one is confirmed.

## Atlas notes

The Therīgāthā, the "Verses of the Senior Nuns," is the oldest anthology of women's poetry in the world — the songs of the first generation of Buddhist women to win awakening, gathered and preserved in the Pali canon. This is among its longest and most famous poems, and its speaker is one of the most remarkable women of the early texts: Ambapālī, the celebrated courtesan of Vesālī, so beautiful and so wealthy that she gave the Buddha a mango grove, and so honoured that she received him on his last journey ahead of the city's princes. Here, old and ordained, she turns her own body — once her fame and her fortune — into the text of a meditation. Verse by verse she reads it: hair once black as bees, now like hemp bark; eyebrows once like crescents painted by an artist, now drooping with wrinkles; hands once bright with golden rings, now like withered red radishes. And each verse ends on the same refrain — *the word of the truthful one is confirmed* — meaning that the Buddha's teaching, that everything conditioned decays, has come true exactly, proven on her own flesh. It is not a lament and not self-disgust; it is a clear-eyed woman watching the Dhamma being demonstrated on the most intimate evidence she has.

## Context

The Therīgāthā belongs to the Khuddaka Nikāya and is among the most ancient strata of the canon; it is the companion to the Theragāthā, the verses of the senior monks. Its seventy-three poems preserve the distinct voices of early Buddhist women — former queens, courtesans, slaves, mothers bereaved of children — and have made it a touchstone for the study of women in early Buddhism and of devotional poetry generally. Ambapālī's own story runs through the canon, from her gift of the grove to her place in the narrative of the Buddha's final days.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

The refrain carries the poem. "The word of the truthful one is confirmed" turns what could have been vanity's grief into insight's verification: aging is not a betrayal of the body but a teaching coming true in it. Read each couplet as a line of scripture being proved — and proved on the most reliable surface there is, because the body cannot lie about time. That a woman once defined entirely by her beauty should be the one to read it this clearly is the poem's quiet triumph.
