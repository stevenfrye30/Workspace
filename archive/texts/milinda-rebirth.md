# Continuity Without a Self: Rebirth (Milindapañha)

## Primary Text

**Selected dialogues** — two of the Questions of Milinda, each reproduced in full. John Kelly, trans., *Milindapañha* (Milinda's Questions) 3.2.1, "Continuity of States" (the Time Chapter), and 3.5.7, "Transmigrating to Another Body." SuttaCentral. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). Kelly's speaker tags ("The king asked:", "The elder said,") are kept; an ellipsis (…) marks a cut within a dialogue. The Pali root text is held alongside in the Archive library under `milindapanha-kelly`.

**Milindapañha 3.2.1 — "Continuity of States."**

The king asked: “Venerable Nāgasena, is the one who arises the same or otherwise?” The elder said, “Neither the same, nor otherwise.”

“Please give me an analogy.” “What do you think, your majesty, when you were a child, young and tender and lying on your back, were you the same as you are now that you are grown?” “Certainly not, venerable sir. That child, young and tender and lying on his back was one thing, and I now grown am another.” “That being so, your majesty, there would be no notion of mother, nor of father, nor of teacher, nor of a craftsperson, nor of a virtuous person, nor of a wise person. … And what would you say if somebody said this to be so?” The elder said, “It was just I, your majesty, who was a child, young and tender and lying on my back, and now it is just I who has grown, and all these states are held together dependent on just this body.”

“Give me another analogy.” “Just as, your majesty, some person might light a lamp, might it stay lit all night?” “Yes, venerable sir, it might stay lit all night.” “Is it so, your majesty, that the flame in the first watch of the night is the same as that in the middle watch?” “Certainly not, venerable sir.” “Is the flame in the middle watch of the night the same as that in the last watch?” “Certainly not, venerable sir.” “Is it then so, your majesty, that the lamp in the first watch of the night is different from that in the middle watch, which is different from that in the last watch?” “Certainly not, venerable sir. It was burning all through the night dependent on just itself.” “In the same way, your majesty, a continuity of states runs on; as one arises, another ceases. It runs on like there was no before and no after. Consequently, it is neither the same nor the other and the last consciousness is comprised of the first consciousness.”

“Give me another analogy.” “Just as, your majesty, milk some time after being milked from a cow would turn to curds, and from curds into butter, and from butter to ghee, would someone who might say, ‘That milk is those curds, those curds is that butter, that butter is that ghee,’ be speaking rightly?” “Certainly not, venerable sir, they come into being because of it.” “In the same way, your majesty, a continuity of states runs on; as one arises, another ceases. It runs on like there was no before and no after. Consequently, it is neither the same nor the other and the last consciousness is comprised of the first consciousness.” “You are clever, Venerable Nāgasena!”

**Milindapañha 3.5.7 — "Transmigrating to Another Body."**

The king asked: “Venerable Nāgasena, is there any being at all that transmigrates from this body to another body?” “Certainly not, your majesty.” “If, venerable sir, there is no transmigrating from this body to another body, is it not then that one would be freed from evil actions?” “Yes, your majesty, if it would not reconnect, then one would be freed from evil actions. But because, your majesty, it does reconnect, one therefore is not freed from evil actions.”

“Please give me an analogy.” “Just as, your majesty, some person or other might steal another person’s mangos, would that man deserve punishment?” “Yes, venerable sir, he would deserve punishment.” “But, your majesty, those mangos that were taken are not the same as those that were planted, why would he deserve punishment?” “Those mangos, venerable sir, arose dependent on the others, therefore he would deserve punishment.” “In the same way, your majesty, with this name-and-form one performs a lovely or an evil action, and by this action another name-and-form reconnects. Therefore, one is not freed from evil actions.” “You are clever, Venerable Nāgasena!”

## Atlas notes

These two dialogues take up the question the chariot left hanging. Having shown the king that there is no separate self behind the assembled parts, Nāgasena must answer the obvious objection: then who is reborn, and how can deeds bear fruit across lives? His answer is the Buddhist middle path between two errors — that a soul passes intact from life to life (eternalism), and that death simply ends everything (annihilationism). The reborn one, he says, is "**neither the same, nor otherwise**." The flame at the third watch of the night is not the flame that was lit at dusk, yet it is not a *different* fire either — it is one burning, "dependent on just itself." So with a person: not even across a single lifetime are the infant and the grown adult the *same* thing, yet they are not strangers — "all these states are held together dependent on just this body." Rebirth is this same process stretched past death: "a continuity of states runs on; as one arises, another ceases … the last consciousness is comprised of the first consciousness." The second dialogue closes the loop on responsibility. *Nothing at all transmigrates* — no being crosses from one body to the next. And yet one is not thereby let off the hook, because a new "name-and-form" (mind-and-body) **reconnects**, conditioned by the deeds of the old, just as the stolen mangoes are not the very fruit that was planted and yet the thief is rightly punished, "they having arisen dependent on the others." Continuity, not identity; consequence, not a soul.

## Context

The *Milindapañha*, the "Questions of King Milinda," records dialogues between the monk Nāgasena and the Indo-Greek king Menander I (Milinda), who ruled the northwest around 150 BCE. The formula Nāgasena uses here — *na ca so na ca añño*, "neither he nor another" — became the standard Theravāda statement of rebirth without a transmigrating soul, and the analogies of the flame, the milk turning to curds, and the seal pressed into wax recur throughout later Buddhist commentary on *paṭisandhi* ("reconnection," the linking of one life to the next). The work is paracanonical — outside the canon proper but so esteemed that the Burmese tradition includes it in the Tipiṭaka. The translation reproduced here is John Kelly's, dedicated to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

The hard knot these dialogues untie is moral: if there is no self, how can anyone be responsible for what a "past" person did — yesterday or in a past life? Nāgasena's reply is that responsibility never needed a permanent self; it needs only unbroken dependence. The thief cannot say "those were different mangoes," because this fruit grew from that seed; you cannot disown your childhood or your past deeds, because this life grew from them by an unbroken chain of conditions. Read the flame slowly: it is the exact image of a self that is wholly real as a process and wholly empty as a thing. Nothing is carried over — and nothing is lost.
