# Heirs of Their Deeds (MN 135)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Middle Discourses* (Majjhima Nikāya) 135, *Cūḷakammavibhaṅga Sutta*, "The Shorter Analysis of Deeds." SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `middle-discourses-sujato`.

“What is the cause, worthy Gotama, what is the reason why even among those who are human beings some are seen to be inferior and superior? For people are seen who are short-lived and long-lived … ugly and beautiful … poor and rich …”

“Student, sentient beings are the owners of their deeds and heir to their deeds. Deeds are their womb, their relative, and their refuge. It is deeds that divide beings into inferior and superior.”

*The Buddha draws the lines concretely:*

“Take some woman or man who kills living creatures. They're violent, bloody-handed, a hardened killer, merciless to living beings. … If they're not reborn in a place of loss, but return to the human realm, then wherever they're reborn they're short-lived.

But take some woman or man who gives up killing living creatures. They renounce the rod and the sword. They're scrupulous and kind, living full of sympathy for all living beings. … If they're not reborn in a heavenly realm, but return to the human realm, then wherever they're reborn they're long-lived.

But take some woman or man who does give to ascetics or brahmins … If they … return to the human realm, they're rich …”

*And the principle again:*

“Sentient beings are the owners of their deeds and heir to their deeds. Deeds are their womb, their relative, and their refuge.”

## Atlas notes

The *Cūḷakammavibhaṅga* answers one of the oldest questions a person can ask: why are human lives so unequal — some short, some long; some sick, some well; some poor, some rich; some born low, some high; some plain, some beautiful? A young brahmin, Subha, puts it to the Buddha, and the reply is the canon's plainest statement of karma as moral causation: *beings are the owners of their deeds and heir to their deeds; it is deeds that divide them into inferior and superior.* The discourse then draws the connections concretely — habitual killing tends toward a short life and harmlessness toward a long one; cruelty toward sickness and kindness toward health; anger toward ugliness and patience toward beauty; meanness toward poverty and generosity toward wealth; arrogance toward low birth and humility toward high; incuriosity toward dullness, and the asking of wise questions toward wisdom. The point is not a rigid machinery — Buddhism insists elsewhere that results are conditioned and never fully fixed — but a moral one: action shapes the kind of being one becomes, and the inequalities of birth are neither a god's decree nor blind fate but the long working-out of deeds. It is, among other things, the canon's answer to caste and to the scandal of unearned fortune: not "this is how you were made," but "this is what action does."

## Context

MN 135 is the standard reference text for the doctrine of *kamma-vipāka*, deed and result, in the Theravada. It belongs with the *Aggañña Sutta* among the discourses that relocate human difference from inborn essence to action: where the Aggañña dismantles the *brahmin* claim of sacred superiority, this discourse explains difference itself as the fruit of conduct. It is among the most cited texts in Buddhist ethics and in every discussion of rebirth.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read it as ethics, not fatalism, and guard against two misreadings. It does not license the cruelty of telling the suffering that they deserve their suffering — Buddhism explicitly warns that one cannot read a person's past from their present pain, and that the workings of karma are among the "unthinkables." Nor does it mean results are fixed and beyond change. Its force runs forward, not backward: *because* you are the heir of your deeds, your deeds now are the one thing shaping what you will become. The question it leaves is not "what did you do to deserve this?" but "what will you make of yourself?"

## Related

- **deed over birth** → On the Origin of Things (DN 27) — the rejection of caste; difference is made, not innate.
- **action follows intention** → Dhammapada, Chapter 1 (The Twin Verses) — "suffering follows the one who acts with corrupt intent," the same logic in verse.
- **the conditionality it works within** → Dependent Origination (SN 12.1–2) — karma operates inside the wider web of conditions, not as an isolated fate.
