# The Conjured City (Lotus Sūtra, Ch. 7)

## Primary Text

H. Kern, trans., *The Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka, or The Lotus of the True Law*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XXI (Oxford, 1884), translated from the Sanskrit; public domain. From Chapter 7, "Ancient Devotion." The full text is held in the Archive library under `lotus-sutra-kern`.

By way of example, monks, suppose there is some dense forest five hundred yojanas in extent which has been reached by a great company of men. They have a guide … to bring the whole company out of the forest. Meanwhile that great troop of men, tired, weary, afraid, and anxious, say: ‘Verily, Master, guide, and leader … we are tired, weary, afraid, and anxious; let us return; this dense forest stretches so far.’ The guide, who is a man of able devices, on seeing those people desirous of returning, thinks within himself: It ought not to be that these poor creatures should not reach that great Isle of Jewels. Therefore out of pity for them he makes use of an artifice. In the middle of that forest he produces a magic city … Thereafter he says to those men: ‘Be not afraid, sirs, do not return; there you see a populous place where you may take repose … there stay in the enjoyment of happy rest.’

Then, monks, the men … think: We are out of the forest; we have reached the place of happy rest. … They think: We are at rest, we are refreshed. After a while, when the guide perceives that their fatigue is gone, he causes the magic city to disappear, and says to them: ‘Come, sirs, there you see the great Isle of Jewels quite near; as to this great city, it has been produced by me for no other purpose but to give you some repose.’

*The Buddha explains:*

In the same manner, monks, is the Tathāgata … your guide … the Tathāgata, to give a repose to the creatures, very skilfully teaches and proclaims two stages of Nirvāṇa, viz. the stage of the disciples and that of the Pratyekabuddhas. And, monks, when the creatures are there halting, then the Tathāgata himself pronounces these words: ‘You have not accomplished your task, monks … the Buddha-knowledge is near … what to you seems Nirvāṇa, that is not Nirvāṇa.’

## Atlas notes

The conjured city is the Lotus Sūtra's fourth great parable of skillful means, and the most searching about the long middle of the spiritual path. A caravan must cross a vast and dangerous forest to reach the Isle of Jewels; halfway, exhausted and afraid, the travelers want to turn back and lose the treasure. Their guide, too compassionate to let that happen, conjures a great city out of nothing — a place to rest, eat, and recover their strength. Believing they have arrived, the travelers stop and are refreshed; and only when their vigour returns does the guide make the city vanish and tell them the truth: *this city I made only to give you rest — the real goal is near; come on.* The Buddha, the chapter says, is that guide; the magic city is the nirvāṇa of the disciple and the solitary realizer — a genuine repose, but not the final destination; the Isle of Jewels is complete Buddhahood. And then comes the Lotus's most startling line, spoken by the Buddha of his own earlier teaching: *what to you seems Nirvāṇa, that is not Nirvāṇa.* Even the arhat's hard-won liberation, the sūtra claims, is a resting-place conjured by compassion — real, restorative, but provisional, made so that the weary would not give up before the end.

## Context

The parable belongs to Chapter 7 of the Lotus and joins the sūtra's other great images of skillful means — the burning house, the lost son, the rain cloud, the good physician. It is also the Lotus's boldest and most contested move: the claim that the goal of the older path, the arhat's nirvāṇa, is not final but provisional, subordinate to the One Vehicle that carries all beings to Buddhahood. The public-domain English is Hendrik Kern's 1884 translation from the Sanskrit.

The translation reproduced above is Kern's; the full text is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Hold the kindness and the provisionality together, or the parable curdles. The city is not a trick played on the travelers: the rest is real, and without it they would have turned back and lost the treasure entirely. But it is not the destination — and the guide's love shows precisely in dissolving it once they are strong enough to walk on. Read "what seems Nirvāṇa is not Nirvāṇa" not as a denial that the disciples' attainment is genuine, but as a promise: there is further to go, and a guide who refuses to let you stop one stage short of the whole.
