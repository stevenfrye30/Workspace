# The Simile of the Mountains (SN 3.25)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Linked Discourses* (Saṃyutta Nikāya) 3.25, *Pabbatūpama Sutta*, "The Simile of the Mountain." SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `linked-discourses-sujato`.

*The Buddha asks King Pasenadi: suppose trusted men came from east, west, north, and south, each reporting a huge mountain reaching the clouds, rolling in and crushing all creatures. With human life so short, what should be done?*

“… But there is no place, no domain for such battles when old age and death are advancing. … When old age and death are advancing, what can I do but practice the teachings, practice morality, doing skillful and good actions?”

“I tell you, great king, I announce to you: old age and death are advancing upon you.”

*The verses:*

Suppose there were vast mountains
of solid rock pressing against the sky
drawing in from all sides
and crushing the four quarters.

So too old age and death
advance upon all living creatures—
aristocrats, brahmins, peasants,
menials, corpse-workers, and scavengers.
They spare nothing.
They crush all beneath them.

There's nowhere for elephants to take a stand,
nor chariots nor infantry.
They can't be defeated
by diplomatic battles or by wealth.

That's why an astute person,
seeing what's good for themselves,
being wise, would place faith
in the Buddha, the teaching, and the Saṅgha.

Whoever lives by the teaching
in body, speech, and mind,
is praised in this life
and departs to rejoice in heaven.

## Atlas notes

King Pasenadi of Kosala — a busy, powerful monarch and one of the Buddha's regular visitors — comes one day from the ordinary business of ruling. The Buddha sets him a problem: imagine four trusted men arriving from the four directions, each reporting a vast mountain, high as the clouds, rolling inward and grinding every living thing to dust; given how short life is, what should be done? The king answers at once, and his answer is the whole teaching: against a thing like that, his armies, his diplomacy, his treasuries are worth nothing — "what can I do but practice the teachings, practice morality, doing skillful and good actions?" Then the Buddha makes it land: those mountains are *old age and death*, advancing on him and on everyone. The verse names the universality with deliberate, royal bluntness — they crush "aristocrats, brahmins, peasants, menials, corpse-workers, and scavengers" alike, sparing nothing; there is "nowhere for elephants to take a stand, nor chariots nor infantry," no defeating them by negotiation or by gold. Death is the great leveller, supremely indifferent to the very ranks a king exists to uphold. And so the discourse ends not in despair but in reorientation: the one who sees this clearly invests where the rolling mountains cannot reach — faith in the Buddha, the Dhamma, and the Saṅgha, and a life lived by the teaching in body, speech, and mind.

## Context

The discourse closes the *Kosalasaṃyutta*, the collection of the Buddha's dialogues with King Pasenadi — exchanges that repeatedly turn the concerns of power toward the realities power cannot touch. It is one of the canon's most vivid statements of mortality's equality, a theme it shares with the *Aggañña Sutta* on caste and with Ambapālī's verses on aging: death undoes every distinction of birth and rank.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the mountains as a thought-experiment in priorities. The king's instant reply *is* the lesson: when the unstoppable is bearing down, only character and conduct are worth anything — and the time to acquire them is now, not at the last. That the discourse is addressed to a man at the height of worldly power is the point: it is precisely such power that death ignores. The urgency is not morbid but clarifying. The mountains are not a future threat to be deferred; they are already rolling.
