# The Noble Search (MN 26)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Middle Discourses* (Majjhima Nikāya) 26, *Ariyapariyesanā Sutta*, "The Noble Search." SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `middle-discourses-sujato`.

*The two searches:*

Mendicants, before my awakening—when I was still unawakened but intent on awakening—I too, being liable to be reborn, sought what is also liable to be reborn. Myself liable to grow old, fall sick, die, sorrow, and become corrupted, I sought what is also liable to these things. …

Why don't I seek that which is free of rebirth, old age, sickness, death, sorrow, and corruption, the supreme sanctuary from the yoke, extinguishment?

*The going forth:*

Some time later, while still with pristine black hair, blessed with youth, in the prime of life—though my mother and father wished otherwise, weeping with tearful faces—I shaved off my hair and beard, dressed in ocher robes, and went forth from the lay life to homelessness.

*The awakening:*

And so, being myself liable to be reborn, understanding the drawbacks in being liable to be reborn, I sought that which is free of rebirth, the supreme sanctuary from the yoke, extinguishment—and I found it.

*The decision to teach, after his first reluctance:*

‘Flung open are the doors to freedom from death!
Let those with ears to hear commit to faith.’

## Atlas notes

The *Ariyapariyesanā Sutta*, "The Noble Search," is the closest thing to an autobiography in the early canon — the Buddha's own first-person telling of why he left home and what he was after. He frames the whole of it on a single distinction. There is an *ignoble* search: to be yourself subject to birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, and corruption, and to spend your life seeking *more* things that are subject to all the same — a partner, children, wealth, possessions, every one of them as perishable as you are. And there is a *noble* search: to see the trap in that, and to seek instead "that which is free of rebirth, the supreme sanctuary from the yoke, extinguishment" — the deathless. He then tells the story without embellishment: how, "while still with pristine black hair, blessed with youth," and against the tearful wishes of his mother and father, he shaved his head, put on the ochre robe, and went forth; how he mastered the deepest meditative attainments under two great teachers and found that they still did not reach the goal; and how at last, seeking the unborn and undying, he found it. The discourse also preserves the famous hesitation that followed the awakening — the new Buddha's first thought that what he had found was too deep, too much against the grain of a world in love with clinging, for anyone to understand — and his decision, after the god Brahmā's appeal, to teach all the same: *flung open are the doors to freedom from death.* It ends exactly where the first sermon begins, with his five former companions reaching the deathless too.

## Context

MN 26 is the canon's primary narrative of the quest and the awakening, and the source of much that the tradition knows of the Buddha's early life: the names of his pre-awakening teachers, Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta; the "two searches" that frame his whole project; and the episode of Brahmā's request that turned a private realization into a teaching for the world. It is the natural prequel to the first sermon (SN 56.11) — the story of how the teacher came to teach.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Take the "two searches" as a question put to you, not only a record of the Buddha's life. The ignoble search is not wickedness; it is the ordinary, perfectly reasonable human project of seeking happiness in things that share our own fragility — and its only flaw is that those things cannot bear the weight we put on them. The entire path turns on noticing that, and looking elsewhere. And keep the hesitation before teaching: even the one who found the way doubted it could be shared. That it was shared at all is, the discourse quietly implies, the reason there is anything here to read.
