# With Bāhiya (Ud 1.10)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Heartfelt Sayings* (Udāna) 1.10, *Bāhiya Sutta*, "With Bāhiya" — the teaching exchange. SuttaCentral, 2021. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The full discourse, with the story of Bāhiya's journey, is held in the Archive library under `udana-sujato`.

*Having crossed the country in a single urgent journey, Bāhiya finds the Buddha walking for alms and three times begs to be taught, refusing to wait:*

“But you never know, sir, when life is at risk, either the Buddha’s or my own. Let the Blessed One teach me the Dhamma! Let the Holy One teach me the Dhamma! That would be for my lasting welfare and happiness.”

*The third time, the Buddha consents:*

“In that case, Bāhiya, you should train like this: ‘In the seen will be merely the seen; in the heard will be merely the heard; in the thought will be merely the thought; in the known will be merely the known.’ That’s how you should train. When you have trained in this way, you won’t be ‘by that’. When you’re not ‘by that’, you won’t be ‘in that’. When you’re not ‘in that’, you won’t be in this life or the next or between the two. Just this is the end of suffering.”

Then, due to this brief Dhamma teaching of the Buddha, Bāhiya’s mind was right away freed from defilements by not grasping. And when the Buddha had given Bāhiya this brief advice he left. But soon after the Buddha had left, a cow with a baby calf charged at Bāhiya and took his life.

## Atlas notes

The Bāhiya Sutta is the canon's most compressed account of awakening — a single sentence of instruction that frees its hearer instantly. Bāhiya of the Bark Cloth was a famous ascetic who had mistaken his own attainment; corrected by a sympathetic deity, he crossed the whole country in one driven journey to reach the Buddha, and would not wait even for the end of the alms round to be taught. The teaching he receives is as short as his urgency demands: *in the seen, just the seen.* Let perception be only perception — no "I" added as the one who sees, no self planted "in" or "behind" experience. With nowhere for a self to stand, there is nowhere for suffering to take hold: "just this is the end of suffering." Bāhiya understands completely, and within hours is killed by a cow — a stark coda the tradition reads as proof that the deepest realization needs no long career, and that the deity's warning ("you never know when life is at risk") was exact.

## Context

The discourse belongs to the Udāna, the collection of the Buddha's "heartfelt" or inspired utterances, in the Khuddaka Nikāya. The formula it contains — *diṭṭhe diṭṭhamattaṁ*, "in the seen, merely the seen" — is one of the most quoted in the canon and recurs in similar form elsewhere (e.g. the *Mālukyaputta Sutta*, SN 35.95). Bāhiya is remembered as the disciple who awakened most quickly, and the brevity of his teaching has made this discourse a touchstone for the contemplative traditions that emphasize bare attention and direct seeing.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the full discourse is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

The instruction is a practice, not a riddle. "In the seen, just the seen" asks you to notice how ordinary perception arrives already loaded — with a perceiver, a judgment, a stake — and to let it be just the bare event instead. The cryptic lines that follow ("you won't be 'by that' … 'in that'") name the result: when nothing is added, there is no self positioned relative to experience, and so no ground for craving or grief. Read slowly, the sentence is a complete meditation manual.
