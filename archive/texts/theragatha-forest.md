# Theragāthā: The Forest and the Quiet Mind

## Primary Text

**Selected poems** — three single-verse poems from the Theragāthā's "Book of the Ones," each reproduced in full (the collection's opening prologue, which precedes Subhūti's verse in 1.1, is not quoted). Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Verses of the Senior Monks* (Theragāthā) 1.1 (Subhūti), 1.22 (Cittaka), and 1.41 (Sirivaḍḍha). SuttaCentral, 2019. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside in the Archive library under `theragatha-sujato`.

**Theragāthā 1.1 — Subhūti.**

My hut is roofed and pleasant, sheltered from the wind:
so rain, heavens, as you please!
My mind is serene and freed,
I practice wholeheartedly: so rain, heavens!

**Theragāthā 1.22 — Cittaka.**

Crested peacocks with beautiful blue necks
cry out in Karaṁvī.
Stirred by a cool breeze,
they wake the sleeper to practice absorption.

**Theragāthā 1.41 — Sirivaḍḍha.**

Lightning flashes down
on the cleft of Vebhāra and Paṇḍava.
But in the mountain cleft he is absorbed in jhāna—
the son of the Buddha, inimitable and unaffected.

## Atlas notes

The *Theragāthā*, the "Verses of the Senior Monks," is the companion to the Therīgāthā — the elder monks' anthology of awakening-poems, and among the oldest nature poetry in any language. Where the archive's other Theragāthā page gives Mahā Kassapa's long hymn to his mountains, these are three of the collection's terse single verses, each a snapshot of the forest life and the mind it shelters. **Subhūti** sits in a hut that is "roofed and pleasant," and the rising storm, far from a threat, becomes an occasion for contentment: "so rain, heavens, as you please!" — because what makes the shelter complete is not the roof but the mind beneath it, "serene and freed." **Cittaka** hears the forest's crested peacocks crying out as a cool breeze stirs, and the wild itself becomes a teacher: their cry "wake[s] the sleeper to practice absorption." **Sirivaḍḍha** watches lightning crash down on the twin crags of Vebhāra and Paṇḍava above Rājagaha — and in the cleft between them a meditator sits "absorbed in jhāna… inimitable and unaffected," the storm breaking around a stillness it cannot reach. Together the three sketch the whole relationship of the forest renunciant to the wild: it shelters him, it rouses him, and it cannot disturb him.

## Context

The Theragāthā belongs to the Khuddaka Nikāya and is arranged, like the Therīgāthā and the Itivuttaka, by length — these three come from the *Ekanipāta*, the "Book of the Ones," where each elder is given a single stanza. Tradition assigns each verse to a named monk of the Buddha's circle: Subhūti, famed elsewhere as foremost in dwelling without conflict; Cittaka and Sirivaḍḍha, lesser-known forest dwellers. The places are real Magadhan geography — Vebhāra and Paṇḍava are two of the five hills ringing Rājagaha (modern Rajgir). The first complete English translation was Caroline Rhys Davids's *Psalms of the Brethren* (1913). The translation reproduced here is Bhikkhu Sujato's, dedicated to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

These verses repay being read as weather reports of the mind. In each, an outer condition — rain, birdsong, lightning — meets an inner one, and the poem turns on the relation between them. Notice that none of the three flees the forest's wildness; the storm is welcomed, the peacock's cry is put to use, the lightning is simply watched. This is the quiet boast of the early renunciant tradition: not that the forest is gentle, but that a freed mind needs it not to be. Read Subhūti's doubled "so rain, heavens!" as the key — it is almost a laugh. Having nothing left to protect, he can let the sky do as it likes.
