# Mencius: Human Nature and Benevolent Rule (Selected Chapters)

## Primary Text

**Selected chapters** — five chapters from across the Mencius, each reproduced in full. James Legge, *The Works of Mencius*, in *The Chinese Classics*, Vol. II (Oxford: Clarendon; first published 1861, rev. 2nd ed. 1895); public domain. Citations give Legge's book, part, and chapter, with the standard modern reference in parentheses. Legge's romanization (Mencius, king Hûi of Liang, Kâo, Tsze-lû), his section numbering, his British spelling, and his punctuation are reproduced as he wrote them. The digital text follows the Global Grey transcription of Legge's translation, verified word-for-word against a second digital witness.


**Book I, Part I, Chapter I (Mencius 1A:1).**

1. Mencius went to see king Hûi of Liang.

2. The king said, ‘Venerable sir, since you have not counted it far to come here, a distance of a thousand lî, may I presume that you are provided with counsels to profit my kingdom?’

3. Mencius replied, ‘Why must your Majesty use that word “profit?” What I am provided with, are counsels to benevolence and righteousness, and these are my only topics.

4. ‘If your Majesty say, “What is to be done to profit my kingdom?” the great officers will say, “What is to be done to profit our families?” and the inferior officers and the common people will say, “What is to be done to profit our persons?” Superiors and inferiors will try to snatch this profit the one from the other, and the kingdom will be endangered. In the kingdom of ten thousand chariots, the murderer of his sovereign shall be the chief of a family of a thousand chariots. In the kingdom of a thousand chariots, the murderer of his prince shall be the chief of a family of a hundred chariots. To have a thousand in ten thousand, and a hundred in a thousand, cannot be said not to be a large allotment, but if righteousness be put last, and profit be put first, they will not be satisfied without snatching all.

5. ‘There never has been a benevolent man who neglected his parents. There never has been a righteous man who made his sovereign an after consideration.

6. ‘Let your Majesty also say, “Benevolence and righteousness, and let these be your only themes.” Why must you use that word -- “profit?”.

**Book II, Part I, Chapter VI (Mencius 2A:6).**

1. Mencius said, ‘All men have a mind which cannot bear to see the sufferings of others.

2. ‘The ancient kings had this commiserating mind, and they, as a matter of course, had likewise a commiserating government. When with a commiserating mind was practised a commiserating government, to rule the kingdom was as easy a matter as to make anything go round in the palm.

3. ‘When I say that all men have a mind which cannot bear to see the sufferings of others, my meaning may be illustrated thus:-- even now-a-days, if men suddenly see a child about to fall into a well, they will without exception experience a feeling of alarm and distress. They will feel so, not as a ground on which they may gain the favour of the child’s parents, nor as a ground on which they may seek the praise of their neighbours and friends, nor from a dislike to the reputation of having been unmoved by such a thing.

4. ‘From this case we may perceive that the feeling of commiseration is essential to man, that the feeling of shame and dislike is essential to man, that the feeling of modesty and complaisance is essential to man, and that the feeling of approving and disapproving is essential to man.

5. ‘The feeling of commiseration is the principle of benevolence. The feeling of shame and dislike is the principle of righteousness. The feeling of modesty and complaisance is the principle of propriety. The feeling of approving and disapproving is the principle of knowledge.

6. ‘Men have these four principles just as they have their four limbs. When men, having these four principles, yet say of themselves that they cannot develop them, they play the thief with themselves, and he who says of his prince that he cannot develop them plays the thief with his prince.

7. ‘Since all men have these four principles in themselves, let them know to give them all their development and completion, and the issue will be like that of fire which has begun to burn, or that of a spring which has begun to find vent. Let them have their complete development, and they will suffice to love and protect all within the four seas. Let them be denied that development, and they will not suffice for a man to serve his parents with.’

**Book VI, Part I, Chapter II (Mencius 6A:2).**

1. The philosopher Kâo said, ‘Man’s nature is like water whirling round in a corner. Open a passage for it to the east, and it will flow to the east; open a passage for it to the west, and it will flow to the west. Man’s nature is indifferent to good and evil, just as the water is indifferent to the east and west.’

2. Mencius replied, ‘Water indeed will flow indifferently to the east or west, but will it flow indifferently up or down? The tendency of man’s nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards. There are none but have this tendency to good, just as all water flows downwards.

3. ‘Now by striking water and causing it to leap up, you may make it go over your forehead, and, by damming and leading it you may force it up a hill;-- but are such movements according to the nature of water? It is the force applied which causes them. When men are made to do what is not good, their nature is dealt with in this way.’

**Book VI, Part I, Chapter XI (Mencius 6A:11).**

1. Mencius said, ‘Benevolence is man’s mind, and righteousness is man’s path.

2. ‘How lamentable is it to neglect the path and not pursue it, to lose this mind and not know to seek it again!

3. ‘When men’s fowls and dogs are lost, they know to seek for them again, but they lose their mind, and do not know to seek for it.

4. ‘The great end of learning is nothing else but to seek for the lost mind.’

**Book VII, Part II, Chapter XIV (Mencius 7B:14).**

1. Mencius said, ‘The people are the most important element in a nation; the spirits of the land and grain are the next; the sovereign is the lightest.

2. ‘Therefore to gain the peasantry is the way to become sovereign; to gain the sovereign is the way to become a prince of a State; to gain the prince of a State is the way to become a great officer.

3. ‘When a prince endangers the altars of the spirits of the land and grain, he is changed, and another appointed in his place.

4. ‘When the sacrificial victims have been perfect, the millet in its vessels all pure, and the sacrifices offered at their proper seasons, if yet there ensue drought, or the waters overflow, the spirits of the land and grain are changed, and others appointed in their place.’
## Atlas notes

Mencius (Mâng-dze, c. 372–289 BCE), honored as the "Second Sage" after Confucius himself, is the thinker who gave Confucianism its theory of human nature — the claim that people are good by nature, and that morality is the cultivation of what is already in the heart rather than a discipline imposed from outside. These five chapters move from that claim outward to its political edge. The book opens (**1A:1**) with Mencius before King Hûi of Liang, who greets him by asking for "counsels to profit my kingdom" — and is at once rebuked: "Why must your Majesty use that word 'profit'? What I am provided with, are counsels to benevolence and righteousness." Put profit first, Mencius warns, and everyone from ministers to commoners will grasp after their own, "and the kingdom will be endangered." The doctrine beneath the rebuke is stated in the most famous passage of the book (**2A:6**): "All men have a mind which cannot bear to see the sufferings of others." His proof is the thought-experiment that has carried his name ever since — anyone who suddenly sees "a child about to fall into a well" feels alarm and distress, not from any calculation of advantage but spontaneously — and from such reactions Mencius reads off the **four beginnings** (or "sprouts") of virtue: "The feeling of commiseration is the principle of benevolence … of shame and dislike, the principle of righteousness … of modesty and complaisance, the principle of propriety … of approving and disapproving, the principle of knowledge." We possess these "just as we have our four limbs." Against the philosopher Kâo, who held that nature is morally neutral, Mencius presses the image of water (**6A:2**): water may be splashed uphill by force, but "the tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards." If goodness is native, then the moral life is recovery, not acquisition (**6A:11**): people hunt for stray "fowls and dogs," yet let the heart itself go missing — and so "the great end of learning is nothing else but to seek for the lost mind." And because the same commiserating heart is what a ruler must extend to his people, Mencius draws the political conclusion that later ages found startling (**7B:14**): "The people are the most important element in a nation; the spirits of the land and grain are the next; the sovereign is the lightest."

## Context

The *Mencius* (孟子, *Mèngzǐ*) records the teachings, debates, and travels of Mencius, a Confucian who lived roughly a century after Confucius during the Warring States period, when rival states warred openly and rival schools — the Mohists, the followers of Yang Chu, the "philosopher Kâo" of these chapters — competed for the allegiance of princes. The book is arranged in seven books, each divided into two parts (Part I and Part II), which is why its passages are cited by book, part, and chapter (so "2A:6" is Book II, Part I, Chapter VI). Mencius's signature thesis, that human nature is good, was disputed within the Confucian tradition itself — the later Confucian Hsün-tzu (Xunzi) argued the opposite, that nature is bad and must be reformed — but it was Mencius's view that prevailed, especially after the Song scholar Zhu Xi (1130–1200) placed the *Mencius* among the canonical Four Books alongside the *Analects*, the *Great Learning*, and the *Doctrine of the Mean*. The translation reproduced here is James Legge's, made for his monumental *Chinese Classics* (the *Works of Mencius* is Volume II); it is the standard Victorian scholarly version and preserves an older romanization (Mâng-dze, Kâo, king Hûi of Liang).

## Reading note

The chapters reward being read in the order given, because they build an argument. Start with the four beginnings (2A:6): notice that Mencius does not *assert* that nature is good so much as point — to the jolt of feeling anyone has on seeing the child at the well, before any thought of gain. That involuntary response is his evidence. The word translated "principle" (and elsewhere "beginning," Chinese *duān*) means a sprout or starting-point: the four are not finished virtues but seeds that must be "developed," and Mencius is blunt that they can be starved — "denied that development, they will not suffice for a man to serve his parents with." Read the water passage (6A:2) as the defense of that claim against the view that we are morally blank: nature has a *direction*, like water's downward pull, even though force can push it the other way. Then 6A:11 turns the whole thing into a practice — if the good heart is original, then losing it is the disaster and recovering it is the work. Finally, read the two political chapters (1A:1, 7B:14) as the same teaching scaled up: a ruler governs well not by pursuing advantage but by extending his own commiserating heart, and the measure of a state is the welfare of its people, before whom even the sovereign is "the lightest."
