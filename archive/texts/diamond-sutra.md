# The Diamond Sūtra (Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā)

## Primary Text

William Gemmell, trans., *The Diamond Sutra (Chin-kang-ching), or Prajna-paramita* (London: Kegan Paul, 1912), rendered from Kumārajīva's Chinese (c. 402 CE); public domain via Project Gutenberg. Selected passages from chapters 1–5 and 32 are reproduced here; the full translation is held in the Archive library under `diamond-sutra`.

*The setting (ch. 1):*

Thus have I heard concerning our Lord Buddha:— Upon a memorable occasion, the Lord Buddha sojourned in the kingdom of Shravasti, lodging in the grove of Jeta. … As it approached the hour for the morning meal, Lord Buddha attired himself in a mendicant’s robe, and bearing an alms-bowl in his hands, walked towards the great city of Shravasti, which he entered to beg for food. … Concluding this religious exercise, the Lord Buddha returned to the grove of Jeta, and partook of the frugal meal received as alms. … and accepted the honoured seat reserved for him by his disciples.

*Subhuti's question (ch. 2):*

“Honoured of the Worlds! if a good disciple, whether man or woman, seeks to obtain supreme spiritual wisdom, what immutable Law shall sustain the mind of that disciple, and bring into subjection every inordinate desire?”

*The bodhisattva's vow, and its paradox (ch. 3):*

“Every species of life … from these changeful conditions of being, I command you to seek deliverance, in the transcendental concept of Nirvana. Thus, you shall be delivered from an immeasurable, innumerable, and illimitable world of sentient life; but, in reality, there is no world of sentient life from which to seek deliverance. And why? Because, in the minds of enlightened disciples there have ceased to exist such arbitrary concepts of phenomena as an entity, a being, a living being, or a personality.”

“Moreover, Subhuti, an enlightened disciple ought to act spontaneously in the exercise of charity, uninfluenced by sensuous phenomena such as sound, odour, taste, touch, or Law. … Because, acting without regard to illusive forms of phenomena, he will realise in the exercise of charity, a merit inestimable and immeasurable.”

*The Buddha is not seen by his marks (ch. 5):*

“What think you? Is it possible that by means of his physical body, the Lord Buddha may be clearly perceived?” Subhuti replied … “It is impossible.” … Thereupon the Lord Buddha addressed Subhuti, saying: “Every form or quality of phenomena is transient and illusive. When the mind realises that the phenomena of life are not real phenomena, the Lord Buddha may then be clearly perceived.”

*The closing gāthā (ch. 32):*

“In what attitude of mind should it be diligently explained to others? Not assuming the permanency or the reality of earthly phenomena, but in the conscious blessedness of a mind at perfect rest. And why? Because, the phenomena of life may be likened unto a dream, a phantasm, a bubble, a shadow, the glistening dew, or lightning flash, and thus they ought to be contemplated.”

When the Lord Buddha concluded his enunciation of this Scripture, the venerable Subhuti, the monks, nuns, lay-brethren and sisters, all mortals, and the whole realm of spiritual beings, rejoiced exceedingly, and consecrated to its practice, they received it and departed.

## Atlas notes

The *Diamond Sūtra* is one of the most influential of the Mahāyāna "Perfection of Wisdom" (*Prajñāpāramitā*) scriptures, prized for stating their teaching of emptiness in a compact dialogue between the Buddha and his disciple Subhuti. Its title, *Vajracchedikā*, means the wisdom that cuts like a *vajra* — a diamond, or a thunderbolt — through every illusion, including spiritual ones. Three moves recur. First, the great vow with its paradox: the bodhisattva resolves to lead all beings to nirvana, yet truly sees that "there is no world of sentient life from which to seek deliverance," because the very concepts of "being" and "self" have dissolved. Second, action without attachment: give, practice, and liberate, but "uninfluenced by phenomena," not grasping at the marks of what you do. Third, the seeing-through of appearances: the Buddha cannot be found in his physical form, and all conditioned things are "a dream, a phantasm, a bubble, a shadow, the glistening dew, or lightning flash." The sūtra's characteristic logic is negation that loops back — a thing is said not to be itself, and is therefore truly called itself — a way of using language to pry the mind loose from its own categories.

## Context

The Diamond Sūtra took shape in the early centuries CE within the Prajñāpāramitā literature, the textual foundation of the Mahāyāna. It reached East Asia above all through Kumārajīva's Chinese translation of about 402 CE, the basis of the public-domain English version reproduced here. It holds a unique place in the history of the book: the copy recovered from the sealed cave-library at Dunhuang and dated 868 CE is the world's earliest complete printed book bearing a date — a woodblock scroll now in the British Library. The sūtra is recited daily across the Chan/Zen, Pure Land, and Tibetan traditions, and its four-line gāthā on the dreamlike nature of phenomena is among the most quoted passages in all of Buddhism.

The translation reproduced above is William Gemmell's (1912); the full text is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Do not try to resolve the paradoxes into ordinary sense — feel where they press. "Liberate all beings, yet there are no beings to liberate" is not a contradiction to be solved but a stereoscope: act with total commitment *and* without grasping at the reality of self, other, or achievement. The negations are tools, aimed at the mind's habit of freezing fluid experience into fixed entities. The closing simile is the gentlest entry: hold the whole shimmering world as a dream, and the grip relaxes on its own.
