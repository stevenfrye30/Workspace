# The Sūtra of the Meditation on Amitāyus (Amitāyur-dhyāna-sūtra)

## Primary Text

**Selected passages** (drawn from the sūtra's 33 sections / sixteen meditations; Takakusu's scholarly footnotes are omitted). J. Takakusu, trans., "The Amitāyur-Dhyāna-Sūtra" (The Sūtra of the Meditation on Buddha Amitāyus), in *Buddhist Mahâyâna Texts*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XLIX (Oxford, the Clarendon Press, 1894); public domain; sourced from the clean Internet Sacred Text Archive HTML of SBE XLIX (retrieved via the Internet Archive Wayback Machine). Takakusu translated from the **Chinese**, the only form in which this sūtra survives (Kālayaśas's version, 424 CE — Takakusu's own note). His English is reproduced verbatim, including his parenthetical glosses. His italic-letter transliteration of the Sanskrit names is rendered in standard form — diacritics as macron-and-accent forms and italic consonants in their standard values, so the e-text's *Râgagriha*, *Gridhrakûta*, *Agâtasatru*, *Mañgusrî*, *Sâkyamuni*, and the term *Darmadhâtu-kâya* appear here as Rājagṛha, Gṛdhrakūṭa, Ajātaśatru, Mañjuśrī, Śākyamuni, and Dharmadhātu-kāya, while digraph spellings such as "Bhikshu" are kept. One transcription slip is corrected: in § 4 the e-text's "Mahâyâna" for the disciple attending the Buddha denotes **Mahāmaudgalyāyana** and is given as such (the word "Mahāyāna," where it means the Great Vehicle, is kept). Obvious OCR punctuation artifacts (e.g. stray mid-word periods) are silently normalized. An ellipsis (…) marks where the text is shortened — at the start of an excerpt that begins partway into its section, or within a passage.

§ 1. Thus it was heard by me: At one time the Buddha dwelt in Rājagṛha, on the mountain Gṛdhrakūṭa, with a large assembly of Bhikshus and with thirty-two thousands of Bodhisattvas; with Mañjuśrī, Prince of the Law, at the head of the assembly.

§ 2. At that time, in the great city of Rājagṛha there was a prince, the heir-apparent, named Ajātaśatru. He listened to the wicked counsel of Devadatta and other friends and forcibly arrested Bimbisāra his father, the king, and shut him up by himself in a room with seven walls, proclaiming to all the courtiers that no one should approach (the king). …

§ 4. When Vaidehī was thus shut up in retirement she became afflicted by sorrow and distress. She began to do homage to Buddha from afar, looking towards the Mount Gṛdhrakūṭa. … When the queen raised her head as she finished homage to Buddha, she saw before her the World-Honoured Buddha Śākyamuni, whose body was purple gold in colour, sitting on a lotus-flower which consists of a hundred jewels, with Mahāmaudgalyāyana attending on his left, and with Ānanda on his right. … Vaidehī, at the sight of Buddha the World-Honoured One, took off her garlands and prostrated herself on the ground, crying, sobbing, and speaking to Buddha: 'O World-Honoured One! what former sin of mine has produced such a wicked son?…'

§ 5. 'My only prayer,' she continued, 'is this: O World-Honoured One, mayst thou preach to me in detail of all the places where there is no sorrow or trouble, and where I ought to go to be born anew. …' … 'Although all other Buddha countries are pure and radiant with light, I should, nevertheless, wish myself to be born in the realm of Buddha Amitāyus (or Amitābha), in the world of Highest Happiness (Sukhāvatī). Now I simply pray thee, O World-Honoured One, to teach me how to concentrate my thought so as to obtain a right vision (of that country).'

§ 7. 'Now dost thou not know, O Vaidehī, that Buddha Amitāyus is not very far from here? … Those who wish to be born in that country of Buddha have to cultivate a threefold goodness. Firstly, they should act filially towards their parents and support them; serve and respect their teachers and elders; be of compassionate mind, abstain from doing any injury, and cultivate the ten virtuous actions. Secondly, they should take and observe the vow of seeking refuge with the Three Jewels, fulfil all moral precepts, and not lower their dignity or neglect any ceremonial observance. Thirdly, they should give their whole mind to the attainment of the Bodhi (perfect wisdom), deeply believe in (the principle of) cause and effect, study and recite (the Sūtras of) the Mahāyāna doctrine, and persuade and encourage others who pursue the same course as themselves.'

§ 9. 'All beings, if not blind from birth, are uniformly possessed of sight, and they all see the setting sun. Thou shouldst sit down properly, looking in the western direction, and prepare thy thought for a close meditation on the sun; cause thy mind to be firmly fixed (on it) … and gaze upon it (more particularly) when it is about to set and looks like a suspended drum. After thou hast thus seen the sun, let (that image) remain clear and fixed, whether thine eyes be shut or open;—such is the perception of the sun, which is the First Meditation.'

§ 17. '… Every Buddha Tathāgata is one whose (spiritual) body is the principle of nature (Dharmadhātu-kāya), so that he may enter into the mind of any beings. Consequently, when you have perceived Buddha, it is indeed that mind of yours that possesses those thirty-two signs of perfection and eighty minor marks of excellence (which you see in Buddha). In fine, it is your mind that becomes Buddha, nay, it is your mind that is indeed Buddha. The ocean of true and universal knowledge of all the Buddhas derives its source from one's own mind and thought. … such is the perception of the images, and it is the Eighth Meditation.'

§ 33. Thereupon the World-Honoured One came back, walking through the open sky, to the Mount Gṛdhrakūṭa. Ānanda soon after spoke before a great assembly of all the occurrences as stated above. On hearing this, all the innumerable Devas (gods), Nāgas (snakes), and Yakshas (demi-gods) were inspired with great joy; and having worshipped the Buddha they went their way.

Here ends the Sūtra of the Meditation on Buddha Amitāyus, spoken by Buddha (Śākyamuni).

## Atlas notes

Of the three Pure Land sūtras this is the most dramatic and the most practical. It opens not in a paradise but in a prison: the prince **Ajātaśatru**, egged on by the Buddha's enemy Devadatta, locks up his own father, King Bimbisāra, and then his mother, Queen Vaidehī — one of the great family tragedies of the canon. It is from that cell, in grief, that Vaidehī becomes the first person to *ask* for the Pure Land: "preach to me of all the places where there is no sorrow or trouble." Where the two Sukhāvatī-vyūha sūtras describe Amitāyus's land and commend faith and the calling of his name, this sūtra answers Vaidehī's "teach me *how*" with a method — **sixteen meditations** (the contemplations of the setting sun, of water and jewelled ground, of the trees and lakes, of Amitāyus and the bodhisattvas Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta), a disciplined visualization that builds the Pure Land up before the inner eye. At its centre stands one of the most consequential sentences in East Asian Buddhism: *"it is your mind that becomes Buddha, nay, it is your mind that is indeed Buddha"* — the Pure Land folded back into the meditating mind. And it ends in radical inclusiveness: the **nine grades** of beings born into Sukhāvatī run all the way down to those who have "completed the ten wicked actions, the five deadly sins," yet are received at the last moment — the seed of Pure Land's promise that no one is beyond rescue.

## Context

The three Pure Land sūtras are the two *Sukhāvatī-vyūha* texts and this one, the *Amitāyur-dhyāna-sūtra* — in Chinese the *Guan Wuliangshou jing*, the "Sūtra on the Contemplation of the Buddha of Immeasurable Life." Unlike the two Sukhāvatī sūtras, which survive in Sanskrit, this sūtra survives **only in Chinese**: Takakusu's own note records that it was "translated into Chinese A.D. 424, by Kālayaśas, a Śramaṇa from India," and his English here is made from that Chinese. Its ultimate origin is debated — some scholars read it as an Indian or Central Asian composition, others as partly shaped in China — which is one reason it reads differently from its two Sanskrit companions. In East Asia it became enormously influential: its sixteen contemplations and its nine grades of rebirth are a staple of Pure Land practice and of Buddhist painting, and the commentary on it by the Chinese master Shandao (613–681) decisively shaped Hōnen's and Shinran's Japanese Pure Land schools.

## Reading note

Read the frame before the doctrine. The sūtra deliberately sets its teaching of light in the darkest possible place — a son's betrayal, a mother's prison — so that the Pure Land arrives not as a reward for the worthy but as an answer to grief. Then notice what kind of teaching it is: not an argument but an exercise. The meditations move from the easiest object everyone can see (the setting sun in the west) toward the hardest (the Buddha himself), training perception step by step. When you reach "it is your mind that becomes Buddha," hold it against the lavish jewelled imagery that precedes it: the point is not that the paradise is unreal, but that the seeing of it and the awakening it leads to happen *in the mind* — which is why the most extravagant cosmology and the most inward mysticism sit, here, in the same sentence.
