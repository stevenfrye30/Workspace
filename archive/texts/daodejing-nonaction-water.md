# Daodejing: Non-Action and the Way of Water (Selected Chapters)

## Primary Text

**Selected chapters** — four of the Daodejing's 81, each reproduced in full. James Legge, *The Texts of Taoism, Part I*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XXXIX (1891); public domain via Project Gutenberg. Chapters 22, 37, 48, and 78 of the *Tao Te Ching*. Legge's paragraph numbers and parenthetical glosses are kept; his commentary and chapter-title notes are not included. (This is the Project Gutenberg edition of Legge's translation, which renders his terms without diacritics — e.g. "Tao" for Legge's "Tâo".)

**Chapter 22.**

**1.** The partial becomes complete; the crooked, straight; the empty, full; the worn out, new. He whose (desires) are few gets them; he whose (desires) are many goes astray.

**2.** Therefore the sage holds in his embrace the one thing (of humility), and manifests it to all the world. He is free from self-display, and therefore he shines; from self-assertion, and therefore he is distinguished; from self-boasting, and therefore his merit is acknowledged; from self-complacency, and therefore he acquires superiority. It is because he is thus free from striving that therefore no one in the world is able to strive with him.

**3.** That saying of the ancients that 'the partial becomes complete' was not vainly spoken:--all real completion is comprehended under it.

**Chapter 37.**

**1.** The Tao in its regular course does nothing (for the sake of doing it), and so there is nothing which it does not do.

**2.** If princes and kings were able to maintain it, all things would of themselves be transformed by them.

**3.** If this transformation became to me an object of desire, I would express the desire by the nameless simplicity.

Simplicity without a name
Is free from all external aim.
With no desire, at rest and still,
All things go right as of their will.

**Chapter 48.**

**1.** He who devotes himself to learning (seeks) from day to day to increase (his knowledge); he who devotes himself to the Tao (seeks) from day to day to diminish (his doing).

**2.** He diminishes it and again diminishes it, till he arrives at doing nothing (on purpose). Having arrived at this point of non-action, there is nothing which he does not do.

**3.** He who gets as his own all under heaven does so by giving himself no trouble (with that end). If one take trouble (with that end), he is not equal to getting as his own all under heaven.

**Chapter 78.**

**1.** There is nothing in the world more soft and weak than water, and yet for attacking things that are firm and strong there is nothing that can take precedence of it;--for there is nothing (so effectual) for which it can be changed.

**2.** Every one in the world knows that the soft overcomes the hard, and the weak the strong, but no one is able to carry it out in practice.

**3.** Therefore a sage has said,

'He who accepts his state's reproach,
Is hailed therefore its altars' lord;
To him who bears men's direful woes
They all the name of King accord.'

**4.** Words that are strictly true seem to be paradoxical.

## Atlas notes

These four chapters circle one idea from four directions: that genuine power in the world is exercised not by forcing but by yielding, emptying, and not-doing. The Daodejing calls the last of these *wu wei*, "non-action" — not passivity, but action so attuned to the grain of things that it leaves no friction. **Chapter 22** states the paradox as a string of reversals: "the partial becomes complete; the crooked, straight; the empty, full," and the sage prevails precisely "because he is thus free from striving that … no one in the world is able to strive with him." **Chapter 37** gives the cosmic version in the book's single most quoted line: "The Tao in its regular course does nothing … and so there is nothing which it does not do." **Chapter 48** turns it into a path of practice that runs opposite to ordinary learning — where the student of knowledge adds day by day, the follower of the Tao *subtracts*, "diminishes it and again diminishes it, till he arrives at doing nothing," after which "there is nothing which he does not do." And **Chapter 78** grounds the whole teaching in its favorite emblem, water: "there is nothing in the world more soft and weak than water, and yet for attacking things that are firm and strong there is nothing that can take precedence of it." Everyone knows the soft overcomes the hard, the chapter notes drily, "but no one is able to carry it out in practice."

## Context

The Daodejing (*Tao Te Ching*), the "Classic of the Way and Virtue," is an 81-chapter collection of short, gnomic chapters in classical Chinese, traditionally ascribed to the semi-legendary Laozi and assembled by about the fourth century BCE. It is divided into two parts — the *Dao* (chapters 1–37) and the *De*, "power" or "virtue" (chapters 38–81); chapters 22 and 37 fall in the first, 48 and 78 in the second. Water is Laozi's recurring image for the Way precisely because it is the model of strength-through-yielding — it takes the lowest place, gives way to everything, and wears down what is hardest. The translation here is James Legge's, made for Max Müller's *Sacred Books of the East* (1891); the public-domain Project Gutenberg edition is reproduced, which prints Legge's romanized terms without his diacritics. Legge's own chapter-by-chapter commentary is omitted here.

## Reading note

Read "non-action" carefully: it is not doing nothing, but doing without forcing — the opposite of the anxious, self-asserting effort that Chapter 22 says actually defeats itself. The chapters reward being held together. Chapter 48's "diminish, and diminish again" is the method; Chapter 37's "does nothing, yet nothing is left undone" is the result; Chapter 22's reversals are the logic; and Chapter 78's water is the picture you can keep. Notice the honesty of the last one: the teaching is not obscure — "every one in the world knows that the soft overcomes the hard" — the difficulty is entirely in the practice. And let the closing line stand as a key to the whole book: "Words that are strictly true seem to be paradoxical."
