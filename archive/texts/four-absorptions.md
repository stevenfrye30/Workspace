# The Four Absorptions (Dīgha Nikāya 2)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Long Discourses* (Dīgha Nikāya) 2, *Sāmaññaphala Sutta*, "The Fruits of the Ascetic Life" — the four absorptions and their similes. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `long-discourses-sujato`.

Quite secluded from sensual pleasures, secluded from unskillful qualities, they enter and remain in the first absorption, which has the rapture and bliss born of seclusion, while placing the mind and keeping it connected. They drench, steep, fill, and spread their body with rapture and bliss born of seclusion. There's no part of the body that's not spread with rapture and bliss born of seclusion.

It's like when a deft bathroom attendant or their apprentice pours bath powder into a bronze dish, sprinkling it little by little with water. They knead it until the ball of bath powder is soaked and saturated with moisture, spread through inside and out; yet no moisture oozes out.

Furthermore, as the placing of the mind and keeping it connected are stilled, a mendicant enters and remains in the second absorption, which has the rapture and bliss born of immersion, with internal clarity and mind at one, without applying the mind and keeping it connected. …

It's like a deep lake fed by spring water. There's no inlet to the east, west, north, or south, and the heavens would not properly bestow showers from time to time. But the stream of cool water welling up in the lake drenches, steeps, fills, and spreads throughout the lake. There's no part of the lake that's not spread through with cool water.

Furthermore, with the fading away of rapture, a mendicant enters and remains in the third absorption, where they meditate with equanimity, mindful and aware, personally experiencing the bliss of which the noble ones declare, ‘Equanimous and mindful, one meditates in bliss.’ …

It's like a pool with blue water lilies, or pink or white lotuses. Some of them sprout and grow in the water without rising above it, thriving underwater. From the tip to the root they're drenched, steeped, filled, and soaked with cool water.

Furthermore, with the giving up of pleasure and pain … a mendicant enters and remains in the fourth absorption, without pleasure or pain, with pure equanimity and mindfulness. …

It's like someone sitting wrapped from head to foot with white cloth. There's no part of the body that's not spread over with white cloth.

## Atlas notes

The "absorptions" (*jhāna*) are the deep states of meditative concentration (*samādhi*) that form "right immersion," the eighth factor of the noble eightfold path. This passage gives their classic description, each stage crowned with one of the loveliest similes in the canon for how it feels from inside. In the **first**, withdrawn from sense-desire, the meditator is filled with a rapture and bliss "born of seclusion" that spreads through the whole body — like a ball of bath-powder kneaded with just enough water that it is soaked through and through, yet not a drop oozes out. In the **second**, thought itself grows still, and a quieter joy wells up from within, "born of immersion" — like a deep lake fed by a spring rising from its own depths, needing no rain or river from outside. In the **third**, even rapture fades, leaving a serene, equanimous bliss — like lotuses that grow and thrive entirely underwater, drenched root to tip in cool water. And in the **fourth**, pleasure and pain alike fall away, leaving "pure equanimity and mindfulness," still and bright — like a person sitting wrapped head to foot in clean white cloth, no part uncovered. Together the four trace one continuous movement: the mind growing progressively quieter, more unified, and more luminous as the coarser pleasures drop away and a subtler stillness takes their place. From this gathered, "purified, bright, flawless" mind, the discourse says, insight does its work.

## Context

The jhāna formula recurs throughout the canon, and this fourfold version with its four similes — the bath-ball, the spring-fed lake, the underwater lotus, the white cloth — is its standard form. The absorptions are no minor technique: the Buddha entered the fourth jhāna as the platform from which he awakened, and on his deathbed passed through all of them in turn. They are the meditative depth toward which the mindfulness practices — the *Satipaṭṭhāna* and *Ānāpānasati* — are designed to lead.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the similes for their exact emphases, not only their beauty. Each marks a different quality of stillness: the bath-ball, a bliss that saturates completely without spilling into restlessness; the spring-fed lake, a joy welling up from within rather than stirred from outside; the underwater lotus, a contentment wholly immersed and self-contained; the white cloth, a purity that covers everything evenly. The progression is toward *less and less* — less thought, less rapture, less pleasure-and-pain — and toward more and more unity and clarity. And note the point of it: the absorptions are prized not for the bliss itself (which the path will also release) but for the gathered, workable mind they leave behind, fit at last for seeing things as they are.
