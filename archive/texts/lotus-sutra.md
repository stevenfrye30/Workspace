# The Lotus Sūtra (Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka)

## Primary Text

H. Kern, trans., *The Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka, or The Lotus of the True Law*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XXI (Oxford, 1884), translated from the Sanskrit; public domain. From Chapter 3, the Parable of the Burning House. The full translation is held in the Archive library under `lotus-sutra-kern`.

*A wealthy man's house catches fire while his children play inside, heedless of the danger. Knowing their minds, he lures them out:*

The man, knowing the disposition of the boys, says to them: My children, your toys, which are so pretty, precious, and admirable … (such as) bullock-carts, goat-carts, deer-carts … have all been put by me outside the house-door for you to play with. Come, run out, leave the house; to each of you I shall give what he wants. … And the boys, on hearing the names mentioned of such playthings as they like and desire … quickly rush out from the burning house, with eager effort and great alacrity.

*Once they are safe, he gives them not three kinds of cart, but to each the same single magnificent one:*

Then, Sâriputra, the man gives to his sons … bullock-carts only, made of seven precious substances … To each of his children he gives several bullock-carts of one appearance and one kind. … He rightly thinks: Why should I give these boys inferior carts, all these boys being my own children, dear and precious? … I ought to treat all the boys equally and without partiality.

*The Buddha explains the parable:*

Even as that man … attracts his children out of the burning house by an able device, and afterwards gives them magnificent, great carts, so, Sâriputra, the Tathâgata … in order to attract the creatures out of the triple world which is like a burning house … shows, by his knowledge of able devices, three vehicles, viz. the vehicle of the disciples, the vehicle of the Pratyekabuddhas, and the vehicle of the Bodhisattvas. …

… considering that he possesses great wealth of knowledge … and that all beings are his children, [he] leads them by no other vehicle but the Buddha-vehicle to full development. … In this way, Sâriputra, one has to understand how the Tathâgata by an able device and direction shows but one vehicle, the great vehicle.

## Atlas notes

The *Lotus Sūtra* is among the most revered scriptures of East Asian Buddhism, the central text of the Tiantai, Tendai, and Nichiren traditions and deeply influential in Chan/Zen and Pure Land. Its two great teachings are bound together in the parable above. The first is *upāya*, "skillful means": the Buddha, like the father, meets beings where they are, offering provisional teachings suited to their capacities — the three "vehicles" of the disciple, the solitary realizer, and the bodhisattva — as a parent promises different toys to coax children from danger. The second is *ekayāna*, the "One Vehicle": those many paths are not the final truth but a single compassionate device, and what the Buddha actually delivers to everyone is one and the same — the great vehicle that carries all beings, without exception, to full Buddhahood. The provocative core of the parable — was the father lying? — is the sūtra's way of justifying the diversity of the teaching: the apparent "untruth" of provisional doctrine is redeemed by the wholeness of the gift it leads to.

## Context

The Lotus Sūtra took shape across the first centuries around the start of the Common Era. It reached East Asia decisively through Kumārajīva's Chinese translation of 406 CE, on which the Tiantai master Zhiyi built an entire system of doctrine, and from which the Nichiren schools later drew the practice of chanting devotion to its title (*Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō*). The public-domain English reproduced here is Hendrik Kern's 1884 rendering from Sanskrit manuscripts, made for Max Müller's *Sacred Books of the East*. Beyond the burning house, the sūtra is famous for its declaration that the Buddha's lifespan is immeasurable and his death only another skillful means, and for its many parables (the hidden jewel, the lost son, the rain that falls equally on all plants).

The translation reproduced above is Kern's; the full text is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Let the parable's ethical puzzle do its work. The father holds out three carts and delivers one — and Sāriputra is asked whether that was a lie. The sūtra's answer is that compassion, joined to a gift greater than the one promised, is not deceit. Read the three vehicles as scaffolding and the One Vehicle as the building: the earlier teachings were never false, only partial, and they were always pointed at this. Held that way, the Lotus reframes the whole of Buddhism as a single education with one destination.
