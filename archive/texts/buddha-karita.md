# The Buddha-karita of Aśvaghoṣa (The Life of the Buddha)

## Primary Text

**Selected passages** (verses from Books I, II, III, V, and XIV of the poem's seventeen books; Cowell's scholarly footnotes are omitted). E. B. Cowell, trans., "The Buddha-karita of Aśvaghoṣa," in *Buddhist Mahâyâna Texts*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XLIX (Oxford, the Clarendon Press, 1894); public domain; sourced from the clean Internet Sacred Text Archive HTML of SBE XLIX (retrieved via the Internet Archive Wayback Machine). Cowell's English verse is reproduced verbatim. His long vowels are given with macrons (so his "Tathâgata" appears as Tathāgata), and his transliteration is otherwise reproduced as he printed it (e.g. "Pushya," "rishis"), with the poet's name given in its standard form, Aśvaghoṣa. The verse numbers are Cowell's; an ellipsis (…) marks omitted verses within a book, and each book is named where the selection moves to it.

**Book I.** **24.** As the queen supported herself by a bough which hung laden with a weight of flowers, the Bodhisattva suddenly came forth, cleaving open her womb. **25.** At that time the constellation Pushya was auspicious, and from the side of the queen, who was purified by her vow, her son was born for the welfare of the world, without pain and without illness. … **33.** Unflurried, with the lotus-sign in high relief, far-striding, set down with a stamp,—seven such firm footsteps did he then take,—he who was like the constellation of the seven rishis. **34.** 'I am born for supreme knowledge, for the welfare of the world,—thus this is my last birth.' … **80.** 'He, the king of the Law, when he has attained to supreme knowledge, will achieve the deliverance from its bonds of the world now overcome by misery, destitute of every refuge, and enveloped in its own chains of delusion.'

**Book II.** **28.** 'He might perchance see some inauspicious sight which could disturb his mind,'—thus reflecting the king had a dwelling prepared for him apart from the busy press in the recesses of the palace. **29.** Then he spent his time in those royal apartments, furnished with the delights proper for every season, gaily decorated like heavenly chariots upon the earth, and bright like the clouds of autumn, amidst the splendid musical concerts of singing-women.

**Book III.** … **30.** 'That is old age by which he is broken down,—the ravisher of beauty, the ruin of vigour, the cause of sorrow, the destruction of delights, the bane of memories, the enemy of the senses.' … **44.** Then the charioteer answered, 'O prince, this evil is common to all; thus pressed round by diseases men run to pleasure, though racked with pain.' … **58.** Having heard these words of the charioteer he was somewhat startled and said to him, 'Is this an accident peculiar to him alone, or is such the end of all living creatures?' **59.** Then the charioteer replied to him, 'This is the final end of all living creatures; be it a mean man, a man of middle state, or a noble, destruction is fixed to all in this world.' … **61.** 'Is this end appointed to all creatures, and yet the world throws off all fear and is infatuated! Hard indeed, I think, must the hearts of men be, who can be self-composed in such a road.'

**Book V.** … **17.** The king's son asked him a question,—he said to him, 'Tell me, who art thou?' and the other replied, 'Oh bull of men, I, being terrified at birth and death, have become an ascetic for the sake of liberation. **18.** Desiring liberation in a world subject to destruction, I seek that happy indestructible abode, isolated from mankind, with my thoughts unlike those of others, and with my sinful passions turned away from all objects of sense. **19.** Dwelling anywhere, at the root of a tree, or in an uninhabited house, a mountain or a forest,—I wander without a family and without hope, a beggar ready for any fare, seeking only the highest good.' … **84.** Then he with his eyes long and like a full-blown lotus, looking back on the city, uttered a sound like a lion, 'Till I have seen the further shore of birth and death I will never again enter the city called after Kapila.'

**Book XIV.** **66.** Thus he, the holy one, sitting there on his seat of grass at the root of the tree, pondering by his own efforts attained at last perfect knowledge. … **68.** He became the perfectly wise, the Bhagavat, the Arhat, the king of the Law, the Tathāgata, He who has attained the knowledge of all forms, the Lord of all science. … **80.** When the Bodhisattva had thus attained perfect knowledge, all beings became full of great happiness; and all the different universes were illumined by a great light.

## Atlas notes

The *Buddha-karita* ("the acts, or life, of the Buddha") is the earliest complete poetic biography of the Buddha — and a landmark of Sanskrit literature, the first surviving *mahākāvya* or "great poem." Its author, **Aśvaghoṣa**, was a Brahmin convert and court poet of about the first or second century CE, and he tells the life not as scripture but as epic: a king's longed-for son, a marvel at birth, a sage's prophecy that the boy will become either a world-emperor or a world-redeemer, and a father who tries to bind him to the throne with every pleasure. The engine of the story is the sequence of encounters the prince was never meant to see — **old age, sickness, death**, and at last a calm wandering ascetic — which break the spell of the sheltered palace and turn his mind toward "the further shore of birth and death." The selection here follows that arc to its end: the birth and Asita's prophecy (Book I), the gilded confinement (Book II), the shattering "sights" (Book III), the meeting with the mendicant and the midnight flight from home (Book V), and the awakening under the tree, where "pondering by his own efforts he attained at last perfect knowledge" (Book XIV). What makes the poem move is that the great renouncer is first shown as a man with a wife, a newborn son, and everything to lose.

## Context

Aśvaghoṣa lived in north India around the first–second century CE, by tradition associated with the court of the Kushan emperor Kaniṣka; he was a poet, musician, and philosopher, and the *Buddha-karita* is his masterpiece. The complete Sanskrit text survives only as far as the events of Book XIV (the enlightenment); Cowell's edition notes that the Sanskrit manuscripts break off there, and the later books in his translation are filled out from the Tibetan and Chinese versions, through which the whole poem was long preserved and beloved across Asia. The work sits in the SBE volume of *Buddhist Mahâyāna Texts*, but it is not a sectarian Mahāyāna scripture: it is a pan-Buddhist life of the founder, read and translated in the Theravāda, East Asian, and Tibetan worlds alike. E. B. Cowell's 1894 English, reproduced here, was the first full scholarly translation into a European language.

## Reading note

Read it as poetry, not as a creed. Aśvaghoṣa writes in ornate *kāvya* — elaborate similes, the moon and lotus everywhere — and the ornament can be enjoyed, but the power is in the plain human turns beneath it: a father's dread of a prophecy, a young man who cannot un-see a corpse, a husband who looks back at his sleeping city and rides away. The "four sights" are not exotic omens but the most ordinary facts there are — that we age, sicken, die — meeting a mind that has been deliberately kept from them; the shock the poem dramatizes is simply what happens when a sheltered person sees the world as it is. Carry the prince's question from Book III as you read: how can anyone, knowing the road ends as it does, stay "self-composed" and heedless upon it?
