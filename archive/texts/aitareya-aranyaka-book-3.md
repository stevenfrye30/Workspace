# Aitareya-Aranyaka, Book 3 (The Upanishad of the Samhita)

## Primary Text

F. Max Müller, trans., *The Upanishads, Part I*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. I (Oxford, 1879), public domain; the Aitareya-Aranyaka, Third Aranyaka, opening of the first Adhyaya. The full translation is held in the Archive library under `upanishads-muller-part1`.

Next follows the Upanishad of the Samhitâ.

The former half is the earth, the latter half the heaven, their union the air, thus says Mândûkeya; their union is the ether, thus did Mâkshavya teach it. That air is not considered independent, therefore I do not agree with his son. Verily, the two are the same, therefore air is considered independent, thus says Âgastya.

## Atlas notes

Book 3 is the strangest and most technical of the three — an Upanishad built on the *samhita*, the "joining" of two sounds in the continuous recitation of the Veda. Where Book 1 expounded a rite and Book 2 the self, Book 3 takes the seam between two syllables and makes it an emblem of cosmic union. A succession of named teachers — Mandukeya, Makshavya, Agastya, Sakalya — debate what the former half, the latter half, and their junction stand for: earth, heaven, and the air between; or speech, mind, and the breath that joins them.

Two images carry the book. One is the body as a "divine lute," the instrument the gods made, of which every man-made lute is only a copy — so that to recite well is to play the original. The other is its closing etymology: Prajapati, the year, having sent forth all creatures, burst apart, and "put himself together again by means of the *khandas*" — the Vedic verses — and that is why the joined text is called *samhita*, "put together." Recitation, on this view, reassembles the cosmos.

## Context

The Samhita Upanishad is the Third Aranyaka of the Aitareya-Aranyaka, in the Rigvedic Aitareya school; it belongs to the older prose Upanishads, roughly the sixth to fifth centuries BCE. It is the most concerned of the three with the mechanics of recitation, and it ends with rules on when and to whom the Veda may be taught. In Müller's volume it is followed directly by the Kaushitaki-Brahmana-Upanishad.

The translation reproduced and described here is Müller's (1879); it is held complete in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read Book 3 as the point where Indian phonetics and Indian metaphysics meet. Its debates look like grammarians' shop-talk — what does the junction of two sounds "mean"? — but the wager is that the smallest unit of correct recitation already encodes the joining of earth and heaven, speech and mind. The body-as-lute image is the key: the text treats faultless recitation not as a skill but as the sounding of a divine instrument.
