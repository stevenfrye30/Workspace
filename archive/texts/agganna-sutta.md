# On the Origin of Things (DN 27)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Long Discourses* (Dīgha Nikāya) 27, *Aggañña Sutta*, "What Came First." SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside this translation in the Archive library under `long-discourses-sujato`.

*The brahmin claim the discourse answers:*

‘Only brahmins are the Divinity's true-born sons, born from his mouth, born of the Divinity, created by the Divinity, heirs of the Divinity.’

*After a long account of how, following a cosmic dissolution, beings descend into matter, grow coarse by greed, and — with the appearance of property — fall into stealing, lying, and violence, the beings do not receive a ruler from heaven. They make one:*

Then those beings gathered together and bemoaned, ‘Oh, how wicked things have appeared among beings, in that stealing is found, and blaming and lying and the taking up of rods! Why don't we elect one being who would rightly accuse those who deserve it, blame those who deserve it, and expel those who deserve it? We shall pay them with a share of rice.’

Then those beings approached the being among them who was most attractive, good-looking, lovely, and illustrious, and said, ‘Come, good being, rightly accuse those who deserve it, blame those who deserve it, and banish those who deserve it. We shall pay you with a share of rice.’ ‘Yes, worthy sirs,’ replied that being. They acted accordingly, and were paid with a share of rice. … ‘Elected by the populace’, Vāseṭṭha, is the meaning of ‘popularly elected’, the first term applied to them.

*And the conclusion:*

The divinity Sanaṅkumāra also spoke this verse: ‘The aristocrat is best among people who take clan as the standard. But one accomplished in knowledge and conduct is first among gods and humans.’ … I also say: The aristocrat is best among people who take clan as the standard. But one accomplished in knowledge and conduct is first among gods and humans.

## Atlas notes

The *Aggañña Sutta* is the nearest thing the Pali canon has to a book of Genesis — but a Genesis with an argument to make. The Buddha tells it to two young brahmins, Vāseṭṭha and Bhāradvāja, who have been reviled by their caste for becoming his followers; the brahmins, he notes, claim to be alone "born from Brahmā's mouth," the one pure class, the rest beneath them. His reply is a long, deadpan, half-satirical myth of how the world and human society actually came to be. After the cosmos contracts and re-expands, luminous, mind-made beings, feeding on rapture, slowly descend into matter — they taste a sweet earth, grow greedy, coarsen into bodies, and with the rise of farmed rice and private fields come ownership, and with ownership theft, deceit, and the rod. Faced with this, the beings do not wait for a king to be sent down from heaven: they *gather and elect one*, "the Great Elect" (Mahāsammata), to judge and to banish wrongdoers, and agree to pay him a portion of their rice. Kingship is thus a contract, made for a purpose, paid for by consent; and the social classes likewise arise from function and agreement, not from any god's anatomy. The discourse ends by demolishing the brahmin premise outright: among those who care about pedigree the aristocrat may rank first, but "one accomplished in knowledge and conduct is first among gods and humans." Worth is character, not caste — and the only birth that finally matters is spiritual: the Buddha's followers, he says, are "born of the Dhamma."

## Context

The Aggañña is among the canon's most discussed texts for Buddhist social and political thought. Modern readers — including the Indian jurist and reformer B. R. Ambedkar, who led millions of Dalits to Buddhism partly on its strength — have read it as an early theory of the social contract: the state as a human institution founded by agreement to remedy disorder, rather than a sacred hierarchy ordained from above, and caste as convention rather than nature. Whether its cosmogony is meant literally or as deliberate parody of brahmanical origin-myths is debated; its ethical and political point is not.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the myth for its arguments, not its astronomy. Each turn lands a blow against the caste order: property appears *before* class, so class is not natural; the first king is *elected and paid*, so authority is not divine; and elsewhere in the discourse the Buddha insists that all four classes alike produce the good and the wicked, the praised and the blamed, so birth cannot fix a person's worth. The frame keeps the aim in sight — two scorned converts — so that what looks like a story about the beginning of the world is really a teaching about the equality of human beings at the end of it.
