# The Jātaka: Wisdom and Folly (Two Tales)

## Primary Text

**Selected tales** — two birth-stories from a collection of 547, the first abridged. From each, the central *Story of the Past* (the birth-story itself) is given; its framing *Story of the Present* and the closing identification of the births are summarized in the notes below, not quoted. Robert Chalmers, trans., *The Jātaka, or Stories of the Buddha's Former Births*, ed. E. B. Cowell, Vol. I (Cambridge University Press, 1895); public domain; sourced from the clean Internet Sacred Text Archive HTML (via the Internet Archive Wayback Machine). Chalmers's English is reproduced verbatim; his footnotes and his bracketed page- and paragraph-markers are omitted, and an ellipsis (…) marks a cut within a tale.

**No. 1. Apaṇṇaka-Jātaka — "The Sure Path."**

Once on a time in the city of Benares in the Kāsi country there was a king named Brahmadatta. In those days the Bodhisatta was born into a merchant's family, and growing up in due course, used to journey about trading with five hundred carts, travelling now from east to west and now from west to east. There was also at Benares another young merchant, a stupid blockhead, lacking resource. … So he sent for the other and laid his view before him, saying, "The two of us can't travel together; would you rather go first or last?" Thought the other, "There will be many advantages if I go on first. I shall have a road which is not yet cut up; my oxen will have the pick of the grass … and, lastly, I shall fix my own price for the barter of my goods." Accordingly he replied, "I will go first, my dear sir." The Bodhisatta, on the other hand, saw many advantages in going last … Accordingly, seeing all these advantages, he said to the other, "Then go you first, my dear sir."

… Now when he had reached the middle of the wilderness, the goblin who haunted it said to himself, "I will make these men throw away their stock of water, and devour them all when they are faint." So he framed by his magic power a delightful carriage drawn by pure white young bulls … with blue lotuses and white water-lilies wreathed round his head, with wet hair and wet clothes, and with muddy carriage-wheels. … "You did well to carry water with you from the other side. But there is no need for it now, as water is abundant on ahead. So break the jars and throw the water away, that you may travel easier." … Such was the folly of that foolish merchant that he did the goblin's bidding, and had his jars broken and the water all thrown away … But as soon as night fell, the goblins came out from their city, and slew every single one of those men and oxen; and when they had devoured their flesh, leaving only the bare bones, the goblins departed.

Now the Bodhisatta allowed some six weeks to pass by after the starting of the foolish young merchant, before he set out. … Here he had his water-jars filled and laid in an ample stock of water; and by beat of drum he had his men assembled in camp, and thus addressed them:—"Let not so much as a palmful of water be used without my sanction. …" … But, as soon as he became aware of the goblin, the Bodhisatta saw through him; for he thought to himself, "There's no water here, in this 'Waterless Desert.' This person with his red eyes and aggressive bearing, casts no shadow. Very likely he has induced the foolish young merchant who preceded me, to throw away all his water, and then, waiting till they were worn out, has eaten up the merchant with all his men. …" Then he shouted to the goblin, "Begone! We're men of business, and do not throw away what water we have got, before we see where more is to come from."

… "We have just been told by some people that it is raining just on ahead, in the belt of forest; now how far does a rain-wind carry?" "A league, sir." "And has this rain-wind reached any one man here?" "No, sir." … "How far off can you see a flash of lightning?" "Four or five leagues, sir." "And has any one man here seen a flash of lightning?" "No, sir." … "These are not men but goblins. They will return in the hope of devouring us when we are weak and faint after throwing away our water at their bidding. … Press on with all possible speed, without throwing away a drop of water."

**No. 38. Baka-Jātaka — "The Crane."**

Once on a time the Bodhisatta came to life in a certain forest-haunt as the Tree-sprite of a tree which stood near a certain lotus-pond. In those days the water used every summer to fall very low in a certain pond, not very big,—which was plentifully stocked with fish. Catching sight of these fish, a certain crane said to himself, "I must find a way to cajole and eat these fish." So he went and sat down in deep thought by the side of the water.

Now when the fishes caught sight of him, they said, "Of what are you thinking, my lord, as you sit there?" "I am thinking about you," was the reply. … "Well, if you'll take my advice, I will take you up one by one in my beak, and carry you all off to a fine large pool covered with the five varieties of lotuses, and there put you down." "My lord," said they, no crane ever took the slightest thought for fishes since the world began. Your desire is to eat us one by one. "No; I will not eat you while you trust me," said the crane. "If you don't take my word that there is such a pond, send one of your number to go with me and see for himself." Believing the crane, the fish presented to him a great big fish (blind of one eye, by the way) … and they said, "Here's the one to go with you."

The crane took the fish off and put him in the pool, and after shewing him the whole extent of it, brought him back again … And he held forth to them on the charms of the new pool. After hearing this report, they grew eager to go there … First of all, the crane took that big one-eyed fish again and carried him off … but actually alighted in a Varaṇa-tree which grew on the bank. Dashing the fish down in a fork of the tree, he pecked it to death … And so he took the fish one by one, and ate them all, till at last when he came back, he could not find another left.

But there was still a crab remaining in the pond; so the crane, who wanted to eat him up too, said, "Mister crab, I've taken all those fishes away and turned them into a fine large pool … Come along; I'll take you too." … Thought the crab to himself, "He hasn't put the fish in the pool. But, if he would really put me in, that would be capital. If he does not,—why, I'll nip his head off and kill him." So he spoke thus to the crane, "You'd never be able to hold me tight enough, friend crane … If I might take hold of your neck with my claws, I could hold it tight and then would go along with you." Not suspecting that the crab wanted to trick him, the crane gave his assent. With his claws the crab gripped hold of the crane's neck as with the pincers of a smith …

"The pool lies this way, nunky," said the crab; "but you're taking me the other way." "Very much your nunky dear am I!" said the crane … "Just you cast your eye on that heap of bones at the foot of the tree; as I ate up all those fish, so I will eat you too." Said the crab, "It was through their own folly that those fish were eaten by you; but I shan't give you the chance of eating me. No; what I shall do, is to kill you. For you, fool that you were, did not see that I was tricking you. …" And so saying he gripped the crane's weazand with his claws, as with pincers. With his mouth wide open, and tears streaming from his eyes, the crane, trembling for his life, said, "Lord, indeed I will not eat you! Spare my life!" "Well, then, just step down to the pool and put me in," said the crab. Then the crane turned back and stepped down as directed to the pool, and placed the crab on the mud, at the water-edge. But the crab, before entering the water, nipped off the crane's head as deftly as if he were cutting a lotus stalk with a knife.

The Tree-fairy who dwelt in the tree, marking this wonderful thing, made the whole forest ring with applause repeating this stanza in sweet tones:—

Guile profits not your very guileful folk.
Mark what the guileful crane got from the crab!

## Atlas notes

These two tales make a diptych on discernment and deceit — and, fittingly, the first of them is the very first story in the whole collection. **Apaṇṇaka** means "the sure thing," "the incontrovertible path," and the tale is built as a contrast between two caravan-leaders crossing a sixty-league desert. A water-goblin, wanting to eat the travelers, disguises himself as a lord just come from the rain — dripping wet, garlanded with lotuses — and advises them to lighten their loads by smashing their water-jars, since "water is abundant on ahead." The **foolish** merchant, judging by appearances, obeys and is devoured to the last bone. The **wise** Bodhisatta, who has gone second on purpose, sees through the figure at once — "this person with his red eyes … casts no shadow" — and, crucially, refuses to act on a claim he cannot verify: he questions his own men until it is plain that no one has felt a rain-wind, seen a cloud, or heard thunder, and concludes, "These are not men but goblins." It is a parable of evidence over appearance, of holding to "the sure thing" rather than a plausible story. In the framing tale the Buddha tells it to credulous lay-disciples who had been following rival teachers, and the foolish merchant is identified as Devadatta.

The **Baka** ("crane") is the obverse: a tale of guile defeated by guile. A crane offers to ferry a drying pond's fish to a finer pool, and the trusting fish — even after one of them voices the obvious suspicion — let themselves be carried off one by one to the fork of a tree and eaten. Only the **crab** refuses to take the crane on trust: he insists on gripping the bird's neck with his claws, and when the crane reveals the heap of bones, the crab simply tightens his pincers and takes the deceiver's head. Here the Bodhisatta is not an actor but the witnessing tree-sprite, who pronounces the moral: "Guile profits not your very guileful folk."

## Context

Both stories belong to the *Ekanipāta*, the first book of the Jātaka. The *Apaṇṇaka* opens the entire collection of 547 — an apt overture, since its subject is the very faculty (clear, evidence-based judgment) by which one is supposed to read all the rest. The "crane and the crab" is one of the most widely diffused of all Indian animal fables, appearing in the *Pañcatantra* and the *Kathāsaritsāgara* and travelling across Asia and into European story-collections; the early translators (Benfey, Tawney, Rhys Davids) traced its migrations in detail. The translation reproduced here is Robert Chalmers's, from Volume I of the Cambridge edition of *The Jātaka* (1895) overseen by E. B. Cowell.

## Reading note

Read both as studies in *how to tell a true thing from a convincing one*. The goblin and the crane are persuasive in exactly the way real deceptions are — they flatter a wish (an easier journey; a better pond) and dress it in plausible appearances. The fool and the fish are not stupid so much as *trusting of surfaces*: they reason from how things look and how good the offer sounds. What saves the wise merchant is not suspicion for its own sake but method — he tests the claim against what can actually be observed, and acts only on "the sure thing." What saves the crab is the refusal to put himself wholly in another's grip without a hold of his own. The pairing quietly sets the Buddhist value on discernment: believe neither the frightening figure nor the friendly one on appearances; ask what you can verify.
