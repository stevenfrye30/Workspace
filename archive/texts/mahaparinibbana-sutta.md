# The Buddha's Final Days (DN 16)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Long Discourses* (Dīgha Nikāya) 16, *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta*, "The Great Discourse on the Buddha's Extinguishment" — three passages from the longest discourse in the canon. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The full discourse is held in the Archive library under `long-discourses-sujato`.

*The Buddha to Ānanda, during his last illness:*

So Ānanda, live as your own island, your own refuge, with no other refuge. Let the teaching be your island and your refuge, with no other refuge.

*His last words, among the sal trees at Kusinārā:*

Then the Buddha said to the mendicants: “Come now, mendicants, I say to you all: ‘Conditions fall apart. Persist with diligence.’” These were the Realized One’s last words.

*The passing:*

Then the Buddha entered the first absorption. Emerging from that, he entered the second absorption. Emerging from that, he successively entered into and emerged from the third absorption, the fourth absorption, the dimension of infinite space, the dimension of infinite consciousness, the dimension of nothingness, and the dimension of neither perception nor non-perception. Then he entered the cessation of perception and feeling. … Then he entered the fourth absorption. Emerging from that the Buddha immediately became fully extinguished.

## Atlas notes

The *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* is the canon's account of the Buddha's death, and the nearest thing it has to a continuous narrative: his last journey north through the towns of the Ganges plain, the meal at Cunda's house that brought on his final illness, the crossing to Kusinārā, and the night he lay down between two sal trees to die. Three moments anchor it. First, to a grieving Ānanda, the instruction that has shaped the tradition ever since: be your own island, your own refuge — with the teaching, not the teacher, as the lamp. Second, the last words, which gather the whole message into a single sentence: all conditioned things fall apart, so do not waste the time you have. Third, the death itself, narrated as a passage up and back down through the meditative absorptions — not a collapse but a composed withdrawal, ending in *parinibbāna*, the final extinguishment of one already awakened.

## Context

The discourse belongs to the Dīgha Nikāya and is the longest single text in the Pali Canon, gathering material that elsewhere appears as separate discourses. It is the source of much of what later tradition knows about the Buddha's last months — the four places of pilgrimage he names, the instruction that the community needs no successor because the teaching and training will lead it, and the cremation and division of the relics that close the text. Its events are commemorated across the Buddhist world, and the sal-tree death scene is among the most depicted moments in Buddhist art.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the full discourse is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the last words as the seal on the whole canon, not as a deathbed sentiment. "Conditions fall apart; persist with diligence" restates the first sermon's truth of impermanence and turns it, one final time, into an instruction: because everything compounded passes — including a Buddha — the only fitting response is undistracted practice. The death-through-the-absorptions is meant to be read the same way: the calm is the point. The teacher whose last act is to enter and leave each meditative state in turn is showing, not just saying, what the path was for.
