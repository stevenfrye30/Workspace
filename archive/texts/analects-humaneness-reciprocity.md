# The Analects: Humaneness and Reciprocity (Selected Passages)

## Primary Text

**Selected passages** — five sayings from across the Analects, each reproduced in full. James Legge, *Confucian Analects*, in *The Chinese Classics*, Vol. I (2nd ed., Oxford: Clarendon, 1893); public domain. From Books IV, VI, XII, and XV; citations give Legge's book and chapter numbers. Legge's romanization (Tsang, Tsze-kung, Yen Yuan, Chung-kung), his sub-section numbering, and his British spelling ("connexion," "vigour," "practise") are reproduced as he wrote them. The digital text follows the Project Gutenberg edition of Legge's English translation, verified word-for-word against a second digital witness.

**Book IV, Chapter 15.**

1. The Master said, 'Shan, my doctrine is that of an all-pervading unity.' The disciple Tsang replied, 'Yes.'

2. The Master went out, and the other disciples asked, saying, 'What do his words mean?' Tsang said, 'The doctrine of our master is to be true to the principles of our nature and the benevolent exercise of them to others,-- this and nothing more.'

**Book VI, Chapter 28.**

1. Tsze-kung said, 'Suppose the case of a man extensively conferring benefits on the people, and able to assist all, what would you say of him? Might he be called perfectly virtuous?' The Master said, 'Why speak only of virtue in connexion with him? Must he not have the qualities of a sage? Even Yao and Shun were still solicitous about this.

2. 'Now the man of perfect virtue, wishing to be established himself, seeks also to establish others; wishing to be enlarged himself, he seeks also to enlarge others.

3. 'To be able to judge of others by what is nigh in ourselves;-- this may be called the art of virtue.'

**Book XII, Chapter 1.**

1. Yen Yuan asked about perfect virtue. The Master said, 'To subdue one's self and return to propriety, is perfect virtue. If a man can for one day subdue himself and return to propriety, all under heaven will ascribe perfect virtue to him. Is the practice of perfect virtue from a man himself, or is it from others?'

2. Yen Yuan said, 'I beg to ask the steps of that process.' The Master replied, 'Look not at what is contrary to propriety; listen not to what is contrary to propriety; speak not what is contrary to propriety; make no movement which is contrary to propriety.' Yen Yuan then said, 'Though I am deficient in intelligence and vigour, I will make it my business to practise this lesson.'

**Book XII, Chapter 2.**

Chung-kung asked about perfect virtue. The Master said, 'It is, when you go abroad, to behave to every one as if you were receiving a great guest; to employ the people as if you were assisting at a great sacrifice; not to do to others as you would not wish done to yourself; to have no murmuring against you in the country, and none in the family.' Chung-kung said, 'Though I am deficient in intelligence and vigour, I will make it my business to practise this lesson.'

**Book XV, Chapter 23.**

Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'Is there one word which may serve as a rule of practice for all one's life?' The Master said, 'Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others.'

## Atlas notes

If the Analects has a center, it is the pair of ideas gathered here: *ren* (仁) — which Legge renders "perfect virtue," and which later translators call humaneness, benevolence, or Goodness — and *shu* (恕), "reciprocity." The two are joined in **Book IV.15**, where Confucius tells the disciple Tsang (Zengzi) that his whole teaching is "an all-pervading unity," and Tsang, asked afterward what the Master meant, gives the classic two-word gloss: being "true to the principles of our nature" (*zhong*, conscientiousness) and "the benevolent exercise of them to others" (*shu*, reciprocity) — "this and nothing more." The rest of the passages unfold that single thread. In **VI.28** humaneness gets its *positive* formulation, the Confucian golden rule: "the man of perfect virtue, wishing to be established himself, seeks also to establish others; wishing to be enlarged himself, he seeks also to enlarge others" — and its method, "to judge of others by what is nigh in ourselves." In **XII.1**, asked by his favorite disciple Yen Yuan (Yan Hui) what *ren* is, Confucius answers with the famous formula "to subdue one's self and return to propriety," binding humaneness tightly to *li*, ritual propriety. In **XII.2** he gives another disciple, Chung-kung, the *negative* form — "not to do to others as you would not wish done to yourself." And in **XV.23**, asked by Tsze-kung (Zigong) for a single word one could practice for a whole life, he names it outright: *shu*, reciprocity — "What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others."

## Context

The Analects (論語, *Lúnyǔ*, "Discussions and Sayings") is a twenty-book collection of short sayings and conversations involving Confucius (Kongzi, 551–479 BCE) and his named disciples, compiled posthumously over perhaps two centuries; modern scholarship places the earliest layers in the fourth century BCE and the received text in the early Han. *Ren* is the book's master-virtue, but Confucius nowhere defines it abstractly — he answers each questioner differently, fitting the reply to the person, which is why these passages put the same disciples' question ("what is perfect virtue?") side by side with different answers. *Shu*, reciprocity, is the one principle he calls a rule for life, and the Analects states it in both directions — the positive (extend to others what you want for yourself, VI.28) and the negative (do not impose on others what you would not want, XII.2 and XV.23). The negative form, sometimes called the "silver rule," predates by centuries the positive Christian phrasing it is often compared with. The translation here is James Legge's, the great Victorian scholarly version, which preserves an older romanization (Tsze-kung, Yen Yuan) and Legge's own British spelling.

## Reading note

Read the two questions about "perfect virtue" (XII.1 and XII.2) as a matched pair: Confucius gives Yen Yuan the inward discipline ("subdue one's self, return to propriety") and Chung-kung the outward conduct ("behave to every one as if you were receiving a great guest … not to do to others as you would not wish done to yourself"). Same virtue, two faces — self-cultivation and treatment of others — which is exactly the unity Tsang names in IV.15. Then hold the two golden rules together. VI.28 is the demanding, positive version: actively work to establish and enlarge others as you would yourself. XV.23 is the restrained, negative version: at minimum, do not inflict on others what you would refuse. Confucius offers the negative form as the *one word for a lifetime* — not because it is lesser, but because it is the rule one can always apply, and the surest place to begin. And notice the method tucked into VI.28: "to judge of others by what is nigh in ourselves." Reciprocity, for Confucius, is not a calculation but an act of imagination — taking your own heart as the measure of another's.
