# Therīgāthā: Songs of Freedom

## Primary Text

**Selected poems** — three of the Therīgāthā, each reproduced in full. Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Verses of the Senior Nuns* (Therīgāthā) 1.11 (Muttā), 2.3 (Sumaṅgala's Mother), and 5.2 (Vimalā). SuttaCentral, 2019. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). Verse numbers are Sujato's. The Pali root text is held alongside in the Archive library under `therigatha-sujato`.

**Therīgāthā 1.11 — Muttā.**

I'm well freed, so very well freed,
freed from the three things that humped me:
the mortar, the pestle,
and my humpbacked husband.
I'm freed from birth and death;
the leash to existence is eradicated.

**Therīgāthā 2.3 — Sumaṅgala's Mother.**

**1.** I'm well freed, well freed,
so very well freed from the pestle!
My shameless husband was most certainly a mushroom,
my mortar wafted like an eel.

**2.** Greed and hate sizzle and hiss
as I squelch them.
Having gone to the root of a tree,
I meditate happily, thinking, "Oh, what bliss!"

**Therīgāthā 5.2 — Vimalā, the Former Courtesan.**

**1.** Intoxicated by my appearance,
my figure, my beauty, my fame,
and owing to my youth,
I looked down on others.

**2.** I adorned this body
all pretty, lamented by fools,
and stood at the brothel door,
like a hunter setting a snare.

**3.** I stripped for them,
revealing my many hidden treasures.
Creating an intricate illusion,
I laughed, teasing those men.

**4.** Today, having wandered for alms,
my head shaven, wearing the outer robe,
I sat at the root of a tree to meditate;
I've gained freedom from thought.

**5.** All yokes are severed,
both human and heavenly.
Having wiped out all defilements,
I have become cooled and quenched.

## Atlas notes

The *Therīgāthā*, the "Verses of the Senior Nuns," is the oldest surviving anthology of women's literature anywhere in the world — poems composed by the first generation of Buddhist nuns, the *therīs*, more than two thousand years ago, and preserved in the Pali canon. Where the archive's other Therīgāthā pages give the voices of age (Ambapālī) and of grief (Paṭācārā), these three sing of one thing: *freedom* — and they are startling in how concretely they name what they were freed *from*. **Muttā** and **Sumaṅgala's mother** both break into almost giddy verse at their release from the grind of a wife's life: "I'm well freed, so very well freed… from the mortar, the pestle, and my humpbacked husband." It is liberation in two registers at once — escape from domestic servitude and the marriage bond, and, in the same breath, escape from "birth and death," the "leash to existence." Sumaṅgala's mother adds the meditator's joy that replaces the kitchen: "I meditate happily, thinking, 'Oh, what bliss!'" **Vimalā** sings a different freedom. A celebrated courtesan, she had used her beauty as "a hunter setting a snare," trading on the bodies men "lamented" over; now, head shaven and robed, she has cut "all yokes… human and heavenly" and become "cooled and quenched." Across the three, the body and the social roles that confined these women — wife, drudge, object of desire — are not denied but *seen through* and laid down, and what remains is a woman, in her own words, simply and wholly free.

## Context

The poems of the Therīgāthā were transmitted orally and gathered into the Khuddaka Nikāya of the Pali canon; the tradition ascribes each to a named nun of the Buddha's time or shortly after. They are arranged, like much early Buddhist verse, by length — the "Book of the Ones," the "Twos," the "Fives," and so on — which is why Muttā's single stanza, Sumaṅgala's mother's two, and Vimalā's five fall where they do. Several of these women appear in the canon and its commentaries as historical figures: Vimalā as a courtesan of Vesālī who tried to seduce the elder Mahāmoggallāna and was instead converted. The first complete English translation, Caroline Rhys Davids's *Psalms of the Sisters* (1909), helped bring these women's voices to the modern world. The translation reproduced here is Bhikkhu Sujato's, dedicated to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Hear how physical the freedom is. These are not abstract declarations of enlightenment but a woman naming, with relish, the pestle she no longer has to lift and the husband she no longer has to serve — and then, without changing key, naming her release from rebirth itself. That is the point: the early tradition did not separate the social liberation from the spiritual one. To be free of the mortar *and* free of saṃsāra are spoken in a single shout of "I'm well freed!" Vimalā's poem is the sharpest mirror: read how unsparingly she describes what she once did with her beauty, and notice that her freedom comes not from shame but from clear sight — the snare is seen for a snare, and simply dropped. The plainness is the achievement.
