# The Discourse to the Kālāmas (AN 3.65)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Numbered Discourses* (Aṅguttara Nikāya) 3.65, *Kesamutti (Kālāma) Sutta*. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The central passage is reproduced here; the full discourse, in which the Buddha applies this criterion to greed, hate, and delusion and to their opposites, is held in the Archive library under `numbered-discourses-sujato`.

The Kālāmas said: “So, sir, we’re doubting and uncertain: ‘I wonder who of these respected ascetics and brahmins speaks the truth, and who speaks falsehood?’”

“It is enough, Kālāmas, for you to be doubting and uncertain. Doubt has come up in you about an uncertain matter.

Please, Kālāmas, don’t go by oral transmission, don’t go by lineage, don’t go by testament, don’t go by canonical authority, don’t rely on logic, don’t rely on inference, don’t go by reasoned train of thought, don’t go by the acceptance of a view after deliberation, don’t go by the appearance of competence, and don’t think ‘The ascetic is our respected teacher.’

But when you know for yourselves: ‘These things are unskillful, blameworthy, criticized by sensible people, and when you undertake them, they lead to harm and suffering’, then you should give them up.”

## Atlas notes

The Kālāmas of Kesaputta tell the Buddha their problem: teacher after teacher passes through, each promoting his own doctrine and running down the rest, and they cannot tell who is right. The Buddha does not resolve the dispute by adding his own claim to the pile. Instead he hands them a method. He lists ten grounds that are *not* sufficient by themselves — oral tradition, lineage, scripture, logic, inference, plausible reasoning, a teacher's reputation — and replaces them with a test: see for yourself whether a course of action, undertaken, leads to harm or to welfare, and whether the wise would praise or blame it. The discourse then walks the test through concrete cases — greed, hate, and delusion lead to harm; their absence to welfare — so the criterion is never left abstract.

This is the canon's most famous passage on free inquiry, and the most often quoted out of context. It is not a license to believe whatever one likes: the test is ethical consequence judged together with the assessment of "sensible people," not private preference. The Buddha closes the full discourse with the "four solaces" — reasons the person who lives with goodwill and clear conduct rests easy whether or not there is a hereafter — grounding the method in practice rather than in doubt for its own sake.

## Context

The discourse sits in the Aṅguttara Nikāya, the "numbered" collection, in the Book of Threes. The Kālāmas were the people of Kesaputta (Kesamutta), a town on the Buddha's teaching circuit; the discourse is named for them, though by its setting it is also called the *Kesamutti Sutta*. Its appeal to first-hand testing has made it a touchstone in modern presentations of Buddhism as compatible with critical and even scientific habits of mind — a reading that is fair to its method so long as the ethical and communal criteria it actually names are kept in view.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's, released to the public domain; the full discourse is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Hold both halves together. The negative half — the ten rejected grounds — is the quotable part, but on its own it collapses into mere skepticism. The positive half supplies the standard: *when you know for yourselves* that something is unskillful, blamed by the wise, and harmful in its results, give it up; when you know the reverse, take it up. The test is not "trust your feelings" but "watch what your actions do, and weigh it with those of good judgment." Read this way, the discourse is a procedure for inquiry, not a slogan against authority.
