# Avalokiteśvara, the All-Sided One (Lotus Sūtra, Ch. 24)

## Primary Text

H. Kern, trans., *The Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka, or The Lotus of the True Law*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XXI (Oxford, 1884), translated from the Sanskrit; public domain. From Chapter 24, "The All-Sided One," on the transformations of Avalokiteśvara. The full text is held in the Archive library under `lotus-sutra-kern`.

If a man given up to capital punishment implores Avalokiteśvara, young man of good family, the swords of the executioners shall snap asunder. … If some creature shall be bound in wooden or iron manacles, chains or fetters, be he guilty or innocent, then those manacles, chains or fetters shall give way as soon as the name of the Bodhisattva Mahāsattva Avalokiteśvara is pronounced.

*From the verses:*

6. If one happens to fall into the dreadful ocean, the abode of Nāgas, marine monsters, and demons, he has but to think of Avalokiteśvara, and he shall never sink down in the king of waters.

7. If a man happens to be hurled down from the brink of the Meru, by some wicked person with the object of killing him, he has but to think of Avalokiteśvara, and he shall, sunlike, stand firm in the sky.

9. If a man be surrounded by a host of enemies armed with swords, who have the intention of killing him, he has but to think of Avalokiteśvara, and they shall instantaneously become kind-hearted.

10. If a man, delivered to the power of the executioners, is already standing at the place of execution, he has but to think of Avalokiteśvara, and their swords shall go to pieces.

11. If a person happens to be fettered in shackles of wood or iron, he has but to think of Avalokiteśvara, and the bonds shall be speedily loosened.

17. He, Avalokiteśvara, with his powerful knowledge beholds all creatures who are beset with many hundreds of troubles and afflicted by many sorrows, and thereby is a saviour in the world, including the gods.

## Atlas notes

This chapter is the fountainhead of the most widespread devotion in East Asian Buddhism: the cult of the bodhisattva of compassion — Avalokiteśvara in India, **Guanyin** in China, **Kannon** in Japan, **Chenrezig** in Tibet. His name is traditionally read as "the lord who looks down" on the world's suffering, and the chapter's promise is total: in any extremity — fire, flood, shipwreck, the executioner's sword, prison chains, wild beasts, demons, witchcraft — whoever calls his name or simply "thinks of Avalokiteśvara" is delivered at once. He is called the "All-Sided One" because he meets each being in the form that will reach it, appearing as a Buddha, a monk, a king, a merchant, a woman, or a child — the thirty-three manifestations — so that compassion is never barred by circumstance. It is this universality and accessibility that made Avalokiteśvara, over the centuries and often in female form as Guanyin, the most beloved figure in the Mahāyāna world, and made this chapter circulate as a scripture in its own right, the "Guanyin Sūtra," chanted for protection.

## Context

Chapter 24 of the Lotus (Chapter 25, the "Universal Gate," in Kumārajīva's Chinese) stands somewhat apart from the rest of the sūtra and was very early treated as an independent text. Avalokiteśvara binds together much of the Mahāyāna held in this Archive: he is the bodhisattva who delivers the teaching of the **Heart Sūtra**, and he is the chief attendant of the Buddha **Amitābha** in the **Land of Bliss**. In Tibet his mantra, *Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ*, is the most recited of all, and the Dalai Lamas are regarded as his emanations. The public-domain English is Hendrik Kern's 1884 translation from the Sanskrit.

The translation reproduced above is Kern's; the full text is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the long list of rescues on two levels at once. As popular devotion it is exactly what it says — a promise of protection invoked by millions in real danger. As teaching it is a map of compassion's reach: there is no situation, however dire or self-inflicted ("be he guilty or innocent"), that lies outside its address. "Calling the name" is the turning of a frightened mind toward compassion itself; and the thirty-three forms make the point that compassion does not stand on its dignity but takes whatever shape will actually meet you.
