# Itivuttaka: The Three Roots and the Two Nibbāna Elements

## Primary Text

**Selected sayings** — four of the Itivuttaka's 112, each reproduced in full. Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *So It Was Said* (Itivuttaka) 1 (Greed), 2 (Hate), 3 (Delusion), and 44 (Elements of Extinguishment). SuttaCentral, 2019. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). Each saying keeps the Itivuttaka's frame ("This was said by the Buddha… that is what I heard"); Sujato's secondary line-break markers (`<j>`) are rendered as spaces. The Pali root text is held alongside in the Archive library under `itivuttaka-sujato`.

**Itivuttaka 1 — Greed.**

This was said by the Buddha, the Perfected One: that is what I heard. "Mendicants, give up one thing and I guarantee you non-return. What one thing? Greed is the one thing. Give it up, and I guarantee you non-return." The Buddha spoke this matter. On this it is said:

"When overcome by greed
beings go to a bad place.
Having rightly understood that greed,
the discerning give it up.
Once they've given it up,
they never return to this world."

This too is a matter that was spoken by the Blessed One: that is what I heard.

**Itivuttaka 2 — Hate.**

This was said by the Buddha, the Perfected One: that is what I heard. "Mendicants, give up one thing and I guarantee you non-return. What one thing? Hate is the one thing. Give it up, and I guarantee you non-return." The Buddha spoke this matter. On this it is said:

"When overcome by hate
beings go to a bad place.
Having rightly understood that hate,
the discerning give it up.
Once they've given it up,
they never return to this world."

This too is a matter that was spoken by the Blessed One: that is what I heard.

**Itivuttaka 3 — Delusion.**

This was said by the Buddha, the Perfected One: that is what I heard. "Mendicants, give up one thing and I guarantee you non-return. What one thing? Delusion is the one thing. Give it up, and I guarantee you non-return." The Buddha spoke this matter. On this it is said:

"When overcome by delusion
beings go to a bad place.
Having rightly understood that delusion,
the discerning give it up.
Once they've given it up,
they never return to this world."

This too is a matter that was spoken by the Blessed One: that is what I heard.

**Itivuttaka 44 — Elements of Extinguishment.**

This was said by the Buddha, the Perfected One: that is what I heard. "There are, mendicants, these two elements of extinguishment. What two? The element of extinguishment with residue, and the element of extinguishment with no residue.

And what is the element of extinguishment with residue? It's when a mendicant is a perfected one, with defilements ended, who has completed the spiritual journey, done what had to be done, laid down the burden, achieved their heart's goal, utterly ended the fetter of continued existence, and is rightly freed through enlightenment. Their five sense faculties still remain. So long as their senses have not gone they continue to experience the agreeable and disagreeable, to feel pleasure and pain. The ending of greed, hate, and delusion in them is called the element of extinguishment with residue.

And what is the element of extinguishment with no residue? It's when a mendicant is a perfected one … For them, everything that's felt, being no longer relished, will become cool right here. This is called the element of extinguishment with no residue. These are the two elements of extinguishment." The Buddha spoke this matter. On this it is said:

"These two elements of extinguishment have been explained
by the Clear-eyed One, the unattached, the unaffected.
One element pertains to this life—
that with residue though the leash to existence has ended;
and that with no residue, which pertains to what follows this life,
where all states of existence cease.

Those who have fully understood the unconditioned state—
their minds freed, the leash to existence ended—
attained to the heart of the Dhamma, they delight in ending,
the unaffected ones have given up all states of existence."

This too is a matter that was spoken by the Blessed One: that is what I heard.

## Atlas notes

The *Itivuttaka* — "the as-it-was-saids," from the refrain *iti vuttaṁ*, "thus it was said" — is a collection of 112 short teachings, each pairing a brief prose saying of the Buddha with a verse that restates it. These four show the collection's two registers. The first three are its crisp, almost epigrammatic style: *"Give up one thing, and I guarantee you non-return."* The "one thing," in turn, is each of the **three unwholesome roots** from which all bad action springs — **greed, hate, and delusion** (*lobha, dosa, moha*). The three suttas are identical but for that one word, and reading them in series is the point: the same simple promise made of each poison, the same path of "rightly understanding" it and letting it go. The fourth saying, on the **two elements of extinguishment** (*nibbāna-dhātu*), is one of the canon's most important statements of what awakening actually is — and notice how it joins to the first three. Nibbāna "with residue" is defined as precisely *"the ending of greed, hate, and delusion"* in a person who is still alive: the arahant who has uprooted the three poisons but whose five senses remain, who still feels pleasure and pain while the fire of craving is out. Nibbāna "with no residue" is what comes at the end of such a life, when "everything that's felt, being no longer relished, will become cool right here." Liberation, in other words, is not a place one goes but a fire that goes out — first the burning, here and now; then, at last, the embers.

## Context

The Itivuttaka belongs to the Khuddaka Nikāya, the "minor collection" of the Pali canon, and is arranged by number, like much early Buddhist material: the "Ones," the "Twos," and so on, by how many items each teaching enumerates. Tradition holds that these sayings were memorized and transmitted by a laywoman, Khujjuttarā, who heard them from the Buddha and repeated them to the women of King Udena's court — which is why each opens and closes by insisting on its own faithful transmission ("this is what I heard"). The doctrine of the two *nibbāna-dhātu* in Itivuttaka 44 became the standard framework for later Buddhist thought on the difference between the *living* liberated one (*sa-upādisesa*) and final passing (*anupādisesa* / *parinibbāna*). The translation reproduced here is Bhikkhu Sujato's, dedicated to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Take the three short sayings as one breath, and feel the pattern they set up: the whole of unwholesome life reduces to three roots, and the whole of the path, stated at its barest, is to "rightly understand" each and put it down. Then read Itivuttaka 44 as the same teaching seen from the far side. The image to hold is in Sujato's chosen word, *extinguishment*: nibbāna is literally a "blowing out," and the sutta carefully distinguishes the moment the flame of greed-hate-delusion is quenched in a still-living person — who goes on feeling pleasure and pain, but without the fever of craving — from the final cooling when even feeling is "no longer relished." It is a strikingly undramatic picture of the highest goal: not annihilation and not a heaven, but a fire that has simply, completely, gone out.
