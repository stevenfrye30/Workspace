# Udāna: The Bliss of Freedom, and the Flame Gone Out

## Primary Text

**Selected sayings** — two of the Udāna's 80, each reproduced in full. Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Heartfelt Sayings* (Udāna) 2.1 (With Mucalinda) and 8.10 (Dabba, the second). SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The Pali root text is held alongside in the Archive library under `udana-sujato`.

**Udāna 2.1 — With Mucalinda.**

So I have heard. At one time, when he was first awakened, the Buddha was staying in Uruvelā at the root of the Mucalinda tree on the bank of the Nerañjarā River. There the Buddha sat cross-legged for seven days without moving, experiencing the bliss of freedom.

Just then a large storm blew up out of season, bringing seven days of precipitation, with cold winds and overcast skies. Mucalinda, the dragon king, came out from his abode, encircled the Buddha's body with seven coils and spread his large hood over his head, thinking, "May the Buddha not be hot or cold, nor be bothered by flies, mosquitoes, wind, sun, or reptiles."

When seven days had passed, the Buddha emerged from that state of immersion. When he knew the heavens were clear and cloudless, Mucalinda unwrapped his coils from the Buddha's body. Hiding his own form, he manifested in the form of a brahmin youth. He stood in front of the Buddha, venerating him with cupped palms.

Then, understanding this matter, on that occasion the Buddha expressed this heartfelt sentiment:

"Seclusion is happiness for the contented
who see the teaching they have learned.
Kindness for the world is happiness
for one who'd not harm a living creature.

Dispassion for the world is happiness
for one who has gone beyond sensual pleasures.
But dispelling the conceit 'I am'
is truly the ultimate happiness."

**Udāna 8.10 — Dabba (2nd).**

So I have heard. At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta's Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's monastery. There the Buddha addressed the mendicants, "Mendicants!" "Venerable sir," they replied. The Buddha said this:

"Mendicants, when Dabba the Mallian rose into the air and, sitting cross-legged in midair, entered and withdrew from the fire element before becoming fully quenched, his body burning and combusting left neither ashes nor soot to be found. It's like when ghee or oil blaze and burn, and neither ashes nor soot are found. In the same way, when Dabba the Mallian rose into the air and, sitting cross-legged in midair, entered and withdrew from the fire element before becoming fully quenched, his body burning and combusting left neither ashes nor soot to be found."

Then, understanding this matter, on that occasion the Buddha expressed this heartfelt sentiment:

"Just as the glow of iron struck
by a hammer in the fire of Jātaveda
gradually dissipates,
its destiny unknown;

in the same way, for the rightly released,
who have crossed the flood of sensual bonds,
and attained unshakable happiness,
their destiny cannot be found."

## Atlas notes

The *Udāna* — the "heartfelt sayings," or "inspired utterances" — is a collection of eighty short episodes, each ending in a spontaneous verse the Buddha "breathes out" (*udāna*) on some occasion. These two frame the spectrum of freedom the collection celebrates. **Mucalinda** is one of the loveliest scenes in the canon: in the weeks just after his awakening, an unseasonal storm breaks, and the great dragon-king (*nāga*) Mucalinda rises from the waters and wraps the meditating Buddha in seven coils, spreading his hood like an umbrella against the rain — an image carved on stūpas across Asia. The Buddha's answering verse is a quiet ladder of happiness: the contentment of seclusion, the joy of harmlessness, the ease of dispassion — and then, above them all, the one that is "truly the ultimate happiness": *dispelling the conceit "I am."* The whole of the path is folded into that last line; the deepest freedom is freedom from the sense of a self that needs defending.

**Dabba** is the very last sutta of the Udāna, and it answers the question the conceit "I am" leaves open: what becomes of someone in whom that self has gone out entirely? The arahant Dabba, at his death, rises into the air and passes away in a blaze that "left neither ashes nor soot." The Buddha's verse turns this into the canon's recurring image for the fully released: like the glow of struck iron that fades in the air, "its destiny unknown," so too the destiny of one "rightly released… cannot be found." It is not that they are annihilated — the Buddha elsewhere refuses both "exists" and "does not exist" of the freed one — but that, craving extinguished, there is simply nothing left to trace. The collection that opens with the bliss of awakening thus closes with its mystery: a fire gone fully out.

## Context

The Udāna belongs to the Khuddaka Nikāya and is arranged in eight chapters (*vaggas*) of ten suttas each. Its hallmark is the pairing of a narrative occasion with a terse verse of inspiration — a form that preserves some of the most quoted lines in Buddhism (including the Bāhiya teaching and the apophatic nibbāna verses held elsewhere in this archive). The *nāga* Mucalinda gives his name to the tree and the lake near the site of the awakening, and the scene became a major theme of Buddhist art, especially in Southeast Asia, where the Buddha is often shown seated on the coiled serpent beneath its hood. The image of the extinguished flame in the Dabba sutta is bound up with the literal meaning of *nibbāna* — "blowing out," "quenching" — and with the canon's consistent refusal to say where the liberated one "goes." The translation reproduced here is Bhikkhu Sujato's, dedicated to the public domain; the Pali root text is preserved beside it in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read Mucalinda's verse as a ranked series, not a list: each happiness is real, but each is surpassed by the next, and the summit is not a special experience but the *subtraction* of something — the conceit "I am." Sit with how strange that is as a definition of the highest happiness: not gaining, but the falling-away of the one who would gain. Then let the Dabba verse complete the thought. Our instinct is to ask where the freed person ends up, as if liberation were a journey to somewhere; the sutta gently refuses the question. The glow leaves the iron and is gone — not destroyed, not hidden, simply no longer findable as a thing. Where there is no "I am" to begin with, there is no "where" for it to go.
