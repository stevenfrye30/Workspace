# The Parable of the Lost Son (Lotus Sūtra, Ch. 4)

## Primary Text

H. Kern, trans., *The Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka, or The Lotus of the True Law*, Sacred Books of the East, Vol. XXI (Oxford, 1884), translated from the Sanskrit; public domain. From Chapter 4, "Disposition" — the parable told by the senior disciples Mahā-Kāśyapa, Subhūti, Mahā-Kātyāyana, and Mahā-Maudgalyāyana. The full text is held in the Archive library under `lotus-sutra-kern`.

It is a case, O Lord, as if a certain man went away from his father and betook himself to some other place. He lives there in foreign parts for many years, twenty or thirty or forty or fifty. In course of time the one (the father) becomes a great man; the other (the son) is poor; in seeking a livelihood for the sake of food and clothing he roams in all directions and goes to some place, whereas his father removes to another country. The latter has much wealth, gold, corn, treasures, and granaries …

*The poor son, wandering in search of food, comes by chance to his father's house — not knowing it is his father's — and the father recognizes him:*

Thereupon, Lord, the poor man quickly departs, runs off, does not tarry from fear of a series of supposed dangers. But the rich man, sitting on the throne at the door of his mansion, has recognised his son at first sight, in consequence whereof he is content, in high spirits, charmed, delighted, filled with joy and cheerfulness. He thinks: Wonderful! he who is to enjoy this plenty of bullion, gold, money and corn, treasures and granaries, has been found! He of whom I have been thinking again and again, is here now that I am old, aged, advanced in years.

*Knowing the son would flee in fear if told the truth, the father conceals it and draws near by a humble device:*

Then the householder descends from his mansion, lays off his wreath and ornaments, parts with his soft, clean, and gorgeous attire, puts on dirty raiment, takes a basket in his right hand, smears his body with dust, and goes to his son, whom he greets from afar, and thus addresses: Please, take the baskets and without delay remove the dust. By this device he manages to speak to his son … Do, sirrah, remain here in my service.

*Only at the last, when the son is mature and confident, does the father reveal who he is:*

The time of his death approaching, he sends for the poor man, presents him to a gathering of his relations, and before the king or king's peer and in the presence of citizens and country-people makes the following speech: Hear, gentlemen! this is my own son, by me begotten. It is now fifty years that he disappeared from such and such a town. He is so and so by name, and myself am so and so by name. … To him I leave all my revenues, and all my personal (or private) wealth shall he acknowledge (his own).

## Atlas notes

This is the Lotus Sūtra's tenderest parable, and its most unusual: it is spoken not by the Buddha but by his most senior disciples — the great arhats Mahā-Kāśyapa and the others — as their own confession of having finally understood. They are the lost son. Long ago they "left the father," settled for a modest liberation, and never imagined they were heirs to anything greater than a quiet nirvāṇa. The father is the Buddha, immensely rich in wisdom, who recognizes his children at once but knows that to announce their full inheritance — complete Buddhahood — would only frighten them off, as the destitute son flees the splendor of the mansion. So the father comes down, puts on dirty clothes, takes up a basket, and works beside his son at humble tasks, raising him by degrees until, at the very end, he can reveal the truth: *this is my own son, and all I have is his.* The "humble work," the disciples are saying, was every preliminary teaching they were given; the lesser path was the father drawing near in disguise, and it led, all along, to the inheritance they could not at first believe was theirs.

## Context

The parable belongs to Chapter 4 of the Lotus Sūtra and answers the burning-house parable of Chapter 3: where the Buddha had taught "skillful means" and the One Vehicle, here the disciples reply that they have grasped it, and prove so by telling this story of their own awakening to their destiny. Its resemblance to the Christian parable of the prodigal son (Luke 15) is often remarked — a son lost and a father waiting — but the lesson is different: this son does not return in repentance and is not even aware he is a son; the drama is the father's patient pedagogy in leading him, step by step, to a greatness he cannot yet conceive. The public-domain English is Hendrik Kern's 1884 translation from the Sanskrit.

The translation reproduced above is Kern's; the full text is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

The heart of the parable is the father's restraint. He knows his son immediately, and longs to embrace him, yet says nothing — because the son could not bear the truth and would run. Love here takes the form of patience and disguise: meeting the other exactly where they can be met, and waiting. Read the "fifty years" and the lowly employment not as delay but as the only road by which the inheritance could actually be received. The gradual path is not a lesser gift; it is how the great gift is made bearable.
