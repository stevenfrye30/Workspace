# The Foundations of Mindfulness (MN 10)

## Primary Text

Bhikkhu Sujato, trans., *Middle Discourses* (Majjhima Nikāya) 10, *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, "Mindfulness Meditation" — the opening, which frames the four foundations. SuttaCentral, 2018. Dedicated to the public domain (CC0). The full discourse, which details each of the four contemplations, is held in the Archive library under `middle-discourses-sujato`.

So I have heard. At one time the Buddha was staying in the land of the Kurus, near the Kuru town named Kammāsadamma. There the Buddha addressed the mendicants, “Mendicants!” “Venerable sir,” they replied. The Buddha said this:

“Mendicants, the four kinds of mindfulness meditation are the path to convergence. They are in order to purify sentient beings, to get past sorrow and crying, to make an end of pain and sadness, to discover the system, and to realize extinguishment.

What four? It’s when a mendicant meditates by observing an aspect of the body—keen, aware, and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world. They meditate observing an aspect of feelings—keen, aware, and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world. They meditate observing an aspect of the mind—keen, aware, and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world. They meditate observing an aspect of principles—keen, aware, and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world.”

## Atlas notes

The *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* is the single most influential meditation discourse in the canon — the textual root of the entire Buddhist mindfulness tradition, and, through it, of much of the modern secular mindfulness movement. Its claim is large: the four kinds of mindfulness meditation are *ekāyana magga*, which Sujato renders "the path to convergence" (other translators: "the direct path," "the one way") — the route by which beings are purified, grief is left behind, and extinguishment realized. The opening reproduced here names the four fields of observation: the **body** (breath, postures, parts, elements, death), **feelings** (pleasant, painful, neutral), the **mind** (its states — greedy or free, contracted or expanded), and **principles** (*dhammā* — the hindrances, the aggregates, the senses, the awakening factors, the four truths). The body of the discourse walks each of these in turn, every section closing with the same refrain: observe it arising and passing, internally and externally, without grasping at anything in the world.

## Context

The discourse is set among the Kurus, a people the canon associates with deep teachings, and it appears in two forms: this Majjhima version (MN 10) and an expanded "Greater" version in the Dīgha Nikāya (DN 22, the *Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna*), which adds a long analysis of the four noble truths. The same four foundations are taken up again from the side of the breath in the *Ānāpānasati Sutta* (MN 118). In the Theravada world the *Satipaṭṭhāna* is recited, memorized, and used as the direct manual for insight (*vipassanā*) practice; in the twentieth century it became, through teachers like Mahasi Sayadaw and the Burmese revival, the basis of the meditation methods now taught worldwide.

The translation reproduced above is Bhikkhu Sujato's; the full discourse, with all four contemplations, is held in the Archive library.

## Reading note

Read the four foundations as one continuous training, not four separate exercises. Each begins where attention naturally is — the body is nearest, feelings next, then the mind's mood, then the patterns (*dhammā*) that organize experience — and each is watched with the same instruction: keen, aware, mindful, and without the covetousness or aversion that turns observing into reacting. The refrain that closes every section ("observing it arise and pass… not grasping at anything") is the actual method; the lists of objects are only where to point it.
